## How this book is organised

The book is a single argument delivered in two halves:

1. **Part 1 answers "what is the market doing?"** — it builds the classical-chart vocabulary: Dow Theory, then the
   reversal patterns, then the consolidation patterns, then gaps, then support/resistance, then trendlines.
2. **Part 2 answers "so what do I do about it?"** — stock selection, stop placement, basing points, position sizing,
   portfolio risk, and the discipline to execute.

The 9th edition adds a third, implicit layer: Bassetti's running commentary that re-anchors a 1940s-1960s text to
21st-century instruments (index shares, futures, options) and 21st-century charts (1987, 2000–2002, Enron, the
dot-com tulipomania, Google).

# Part 1 — Technical Theory

## Chapter 1 — The Technical Approach to Trading and Investing (pp. 3–8)

Sets up the fundamental-vs-technical split and defines the discipline. **Technical Analysis** is "the science of
recording, usually in graphic form, the actual history of trading (price changes, volume of transactions, etc.) in a
certain stock or in 'the Averages' and then deducing from that pictured history the probable future trend." The
argument against intrinsic value is empirical: U.S. Steel traded at $261 in 1929 and $22 in 1932 while its book value
barely moved. Price is set *solely* by supply and demand, which already contains every fundamental fact plus all the
hopes, fears and necessities that no statistic captures.

- Three schools exist — fundamental/statistical, technical, and cyclical; the pure fundamentalist is "a very rare bird."
- The market looks *forward*; fundamental statistics are "past history, already out of date and sterile."
- The whole method rests on one empirical claim: **prices move in trends, and trends persist until the supply–demand
  balance changes** — and those changes are detectable in the market's own action.
- Patterns are "not infallible … but the odds are definitely in their favor."
- A technician could in theory trade a chart knowing only the ticker symbol (not recommended, but the claim is the point).
- Endnote quotes Robert Prechter: fundamental analysis needs *two* forecasts (the data, then the market's reaction);
  technical analysis needs one. Cites Barron's: the 10 DJIA stocks with the *worst* earnings estimates returned +142.5%
  over ten years vs +40.5% for the best.
- Bassetti's framing: Edwards & Magee are "the embodiment and definition of *classical* technical analysis," as opposed
  to the number-driven schools that arrived with the computer.

## Chapter 2 — Charts (pp. 9–12)

The working tools. Only the simplest chart is needed: a **daily bar** of high, low and close plus a volume histogram.
The opening price is deliberately omitted — Edwards found it has no forecasting value (Bassetti dissents in a footnote:
some technicians use it, and candlestick charting is built on it). The closing price matters because it is "the final
evaluation of the stock made by the market during the day" and the figure most traders plan tomorrow from.

- Chart types catalogued: monthly, weekly, daily, hourly, transaction, point-and-figure, candlestick, oscillators.
- **Arithmetic scale**: equal vertical distance = equal dollars. **Semi-logarithmic (ratio/percentage) scale**: equal
  vertical distance = equal percentage. The authors recommend semi-log.
- Semi-log advantages: direct comparison of high- and low-priced stocks, easier stop placement, and it *expands*
  important formations built at low price levels that arithmetic scaling hides.
- Area patterns look much the same on either scale; **trendlines develop more usefully on ratio scale**.
- Weekly and monthly ("long-term" or "major") charts are used chiefly for major support/resistance and long-term trends.
- Warning against mechanical, single-index systems: they are "often confusing and sometimes downright deceptive at a
  most critical juncture."
- "There is nothing wrong with charts — the trouble is with the chartists." It is not the chart but the interpretation.
- Bassetti's caution on modern software: multi-year log charts compress at the top, so break long ranges into shorter
  windows (five one-year charts as well as the five-year chart) to keep perspective.

## Chapter 3 — The Dow Theory (pp. 13–24)

"The granddaddy of all technical market studies." Charles H. Dow's editorials, organised into a theory over 27 years by
his successor William P. Hamilton. Dow conceived it as a *barometer of general business*, not a trading tool. It uses
only the Industrial and Rail (now Transportation) averages — never the Utility or Composite. This is the chapter's
canonical content: **the twelve tenets**.

**The twelve tenets**

1. **The Averages Discount Everything** — except acts of God, and even those are appraised quickly.
2. **The Three Trends** — Primary (a year or more, >20% move), Secondary (3 weeks to months), Minor (usually <6 days).
3. **The Primary Trend** — up while each rally and each secondary bottom is higher than the last (Bull Market); down on
   successively lower tops and bottoms (Bear Market).
4. **The Secondary Trends** — retrace **one-third to two-thirds** of the preceding primary swing, most near the halfway
   mark; must last ≥3 weeks and retrace ≥⅓ to rank as a true secondary. The hardest thing in Dow Theory to judge.
5. **The Minor Trends** — meaningless individually; the *only* trend that can be manipulated at all.
6. **The Bull Market's three phases** — accumulation (news still bad, public absent) → steady advance on improving
   earnings (*the technician's best harvest*) → public frenzy, "cats and dogs" whirled up while top-grade issues stop
   following.
7. **The Bear Market's three phases** — distribution (begins in the late bull) → **panic** (near-vertical drop, climactic
   volume) → discouraged/distress selling, slower, concentrated in the better-grade issues held to the last. "The Bear
   Market ends when everything in the way of possible bad news … has been discounted, and it is usually over before all
   the bad news is 'out.'"
8. **The Two Averages Must Confirm** — no valid trend-change signal from one average alone. Most questioned, most
   durable. They need not confirm on the same day.
9. **"Volume Goes with the Trend"** — activity expands in the direction of the primary trend. Collateral evidence only;
   conclusive signals come from price.
10. **"Lines" May Substitute for Secondaries** — a 2–3 week (sometimes months) sideways range inside ~5%; a break up is
    bullish, down is bearish; longer and narrower = more significant. The individual-stock analogue is the **Rectangle**.
11. **Only Closing Prices Used** — intraday extremes are ignored; any penetration, even 0.01, counts (the authors reject
    the proposed 1.00-point filter).
12. **A Trend Is Assumed to Continue Until Reversal Is Definitely Signaled** — a warning against jumping the gun, not an
    excuse for delay. Corollary: a reversal can occur any time after a trend is confirmed.

- The famous analogy: **tide (primary) · wave (secondary) · ripple (minor)**, and the shore-dweller driving a stake at
  each wave's high-water mark — which is, simplified, exactly what the Dow theorist does.
- Bassetti's 9th-edition revision: 30 stocks can no longer represent the economy. He argues the broad market trend must
  now be read from the **Dow Industrials + S&P 500 + NASDAQ Composite** together, requiring "harmonic convergence" among
  the three; disagreement among them is itself a signal to hedge. He also urges running the **Magee Evaluative Index**
  (Ch 38) across all three.

## Chapter 4 — The Dow Theory in Practice (pp. 25–40)

A worked, blow-by-blow application of the theory to the averages from 1941 through the 1946 top: the 1942 action, the
bull-market signal, the 1943 secondary correction, bull market reaffirmed, spring 1946, third-phase symptoms, and the
bear-market signal. Largely a case study in reading confirmation, non-confirmation and line formations in real time.

- Demonstrates that signals look obvious in hindsight and ambiguous while forming.
- Shows the practical use of tenet 11 (closing prices only) — a June 1946 incident is cited as decisive evidence for
  the orthodox "any penetration whatever" rule.
- The exercise is as much about *patience* during ambiguity as about the rules.

## Chapter 5 — The Dow Theory's Defects (pp. 41–44)

An honest audit. Four criticisms: it invites second-guessing; it is "too late"; its long-run record needs stating; and
it gives little help in intermediate-term trading. The authors concede the lateness — a Dow signal arrives after the turn
by definition — and answer that the method's value is in avoiding large losses, not catching extremes.

- Includes **Table 1: The Dow Theory's 60-Year Record**.
- Critically: Dow Theory does not tell you *which stocks* to trade — which is the gap the rest of the book fills.
- The "too late" objection is reframed: the odds favour the one who waits for certainty over the one who acts early.

## Chapter 5.1 — The Dow Theory in the 20th and 21st Centuries (pp. 45–54) · *Bassetti*

Updates the Dow record to 2005 and reconsiders the theory's standing. The headline numbers, compounded to 31 Dec 2004:

| Strategy | Result |
|---|---|
| Buy and hold | **$39,685** |
| Dow Theory, long only | **$347,895** |
| Dow Theory, long **and** short | **$1,914,490** |

- The risk characteristics of Dow Theory investing are "unique" — the long/short version multiplies buy-and-hold ~48×.
- Argues the result is now directly implementable: you can trade the averages as stocks (DIA, SPY, QQQQ), which was
  impossible or prohibitively expensive in Magee's day.
- Supports Bassetti's broader thesis that even long-term investors should not sit through bear markets.

## Chapter 6 — Important Reversal Patterns (pp. 55–74)

The heart of the book begins. Why reversals take time: a worked narrative of a syndicate accumulating 20,000 shares at
40, marking up to 60–65, then *distributing* — which cannot be done at once without killing the demand. Distribution
therefore requires time, volume and turnover, and it leaves a recognisable shape. Enter the **Head-and-Shoulders Top**,
"one of the more common and, by all odds, the most reliable of the Major Reversal Patterns."

**The four essential conditions (A–D)**

- **A — Left shoulder**: strong rally climaxing an extensive advance on very heavy volume, then a minor recession on
  much lighter volume.
- **B — Head**: a second high-volume advance *above* the left shoulder, then a reaction on less volume back to near the
  previous recession low — in any case *below the top of the left shoulder*.
- **C — Right shoulder**: a third rally on *decidedly less* volume, failing to reach the head.
- **D — Confirmation/breakout**: a close below the **neckline** (drawn across the two intervening bottoms) by roughly
  **3% of the stock's price**.

**Other key points**

- Volume is *relative* — "high" means high for that stock in that period, not an absolute share count.
- Volume at the head is not a reliable tell: roughly ⅓ of confirmed cases show more volume on the left shoulder, ⅓ about
  equal, ⅓ more on the head.
- Early warnings: a conspicuous volume drop on the head; prices falling *below the left-shoulder top* on the reaction
  from the head (tab the chart "double red").
- Many traders sell as soon as a low-volume right shoulder completes, without waiting for D.
- About **20%** of developing H&S patterns are "saved" before the neckline breaks. True false breakouts are "extremely
  rare" — and an H&S that fails is itself a warning that a genuine turn is near.
- **The measuring formula**: measure vertically from the top of the head to the neckline; project that distance down from
  the point of neckline penetration. That is the **minimum** objective.
- **Crucial qualification**: "a Reversal Pattern has to have something to reverse." Take the *smaller* of (the measured
  objective) and (the extent of the preceding advance).
- Necklines may slope up or down; symmetry is a tendency, not a requirement. A down-sloping neckline means a weaker
  situation — but much of that weakness is already spent by the time the neckline breaks.
- **Pullback** to the neckline after the break is common; whether it occurs depends largely on the general market. It is
  the short-seller's second chance and his stop-placement reference.
- Relation to Dow Theory: head → neckline → right shoulder → break *is* a sequence of lower tops and lower bottoms. The
  H&S is Dow Theory applied to one stock — but with earlier warning, no minimum time requirement, and no need for
  confirmation by a second average.

## Chapter 7 — Important Reversal Patterns, Continued (pp. 75–98)

Bottoms, multiples, and rounding turns.

**Head-and-Shoulders Bottom** (Bassetti renames it the **"Kilroy Bottom"** — left hand, nose, right hand, fenceline —
arguing the standard term is absurd). The price pattern is the top inverted; the *volume* pattern is not.

- **The essential difference is volume**: activity must build through the head and the rally from the right shoulder,
  and there **must be a conspicuous burst of volume on the neckline penetration**. Without it, don't trust the breakout.
- The governing principle: *"It takes buying to put stocks up, but they can fall of their own weight."* A low-volume
  break *down* through a top's neckline is trusted; a low-volume break *up* from a bottom is not.
- Bottoms are generally longer and flatter than tops, with lower overall volume and more rounded turns. Major bottoms
  take months; tops can complete quickly.
- Measuring implications and pullback frequency are the same as for tops.

**Multiple / Complex Head-and-Shoulders** — doubled or proliferated shoulders and/or heads. More frequent at bottoms
than tops; rarer at intermediate reversals.

- Symmetry is *stronger* here and can be counted on in trading: the right half is usually a mirror of the left.
- Necklines are almost always near-horizontal; often there is an inner and an **outer** neckline — wait for the outer.
- Counter-intuitively, complex patterns show **less** immediate power: same minimum measuring rule, but the first swing
  is leisurely and rarely exceeds the bare minimum.
- "False moves are relatively rare with both" — and a complex failure, like a simple one, warns that the real reversal
  is near.

**Rounding Turns** — Bowl / Saucer (bottoms), Inverted Bowl (tops). A gradual, symmetrical shift in the supply–demand
balance rather than a struggle.

- Volume at a **rounding bottom** is as clean as the price: it ebbs to an *extreme low* at dead centre, then accelerates
  with the turn, often tracing an arc that parallels the price bowl. These "nearly always denote a change in Primary
  Trend and an extensive advance yet to come" — but the advance is slow and interruption-prone, not a skyrocket.
- **Premature breakouts** out of a saucer are common and not dangerous; prices usually drop back into the channel.
- Volume at a **rounding top** is seldom clean — high and irregular throughout; warnings appear only as the downtrend
  accelerates.
- Rounding bottoms favour low-priced stocks; rounding tops appear almost only in high-priced AA-rated commons and
  high-grade preferreds (where demand depends on slow-moving funds and interest rates).
- **No measuring formula** exists for rounding turns, "but they almost never deceive."
- **Dormant Bottom** — the extreme flat-bottomed variant in very thin stocks: weeks of no sales, a "fly-specked" chart,
  then a sudden inexplicable flurry. Insiders "hold a basket under it" rather than bid it up. It signals an important
  accumulation pattern.
- All these patterns appear on weekly and monthly charts with identical significance and proportionate importance;
  volume detail is harder to read there but less critical. H&S tops are "particularly plentiful on monthly charts."

## Chapter 8 — Important Reversal Patterns: The Triangles (pp. 99–128)

An honest preamble first: no method buys the bottom or sells the top. "The more experienced an investor, the less
concerned he is with getting the last point." Triangles are included among reversals because they *sometimes* reverse —
but **about three out of four are consolidations** (Schabacker's "continuation patterns").

**Symmetrical Triangle** (a.k.a. the "Coil")

- Successively smaller swings: each minor top lower, each minor bottom higher. Bounded by a down-slanting top line and
  an up-slanting bottom line converging to an **apex**.
- **You need four minor reversals to draw it** — two tops and two bottoms — because it takes two points to fix a line.
- Volume diminishes irregularly but noticeably toward the apex, then expands on the breakout.
- **No directional clue** until the break. Odds favour continuation of the prior trend; other charts in the book supply
  collateral evidence.
- **Best breakouts occur between half and three-quarters of the way from base to apex.** Push further and the pattern
  "begins to lose its efficacy"; squeeze into the apex and you get an "end run" and a useless chart.
- Boundaries sometimes must be **redrawn** mid-formation.
- Breakout rules mirror H&S: ~3% close beyond the line; **upside breaks require volume confirmation, downside breaks do
  not**.
- **A high-volume downside break is suspect** — especially out near the apex, it is often a 2–3 day shakeout that
  reverses into a genuine up-move. (Though it can double-cross and keep falling.)
- "Symmetrical Triangles are subject to false moves to a far greater extent than the Head-and-Shoulders or any of the
  other formations" — the worst offender in the book. But more than two-thirds behave.
- Pullbacks/throwbacks after the break happen, but less often than with H&S.
- Rationale: round-figure profit-taking meets new money entering on reactions, repeatedly, until one side is exhausted.

**Right-Angle Triangles** — one boundary horizontal, the other slanting to meet it. "They give advance notice of their
intentions."

- **Ascending Triangle**: horizontal *supply* line on top, rising *demand* line below. A planned distribution of a block
  at a fixed price meeting persistent, rising demand. When the supply is absorbed, prices leap. Once the demand line is
  visibly rising, "the odds are certainly somewhere in the neighborhood of **9–1** that the new buyers will eventually
  have the best of it."
- **Descending Triangle**: horizontal *demand* line below, falling *supply* line above. A syndicate accumulating at a
  fixed price below the market; when its orders fill, the critical line breaks and holders who had never considered
  selling are shaken out.
- Failures are "surprisingly rare." A failed ascending triangle usually becomes a **Rectangle** or a **Double Bottom**.
- Volume behaves as in the symmetrical type; upside breaks need volume, downside breaks need not. Pullbacks are fairly
  common and depend on the general market.
- False moves are considerably rarer than from symmetrical triangles.
- **Ex-dividend adjustment**: when a stock goes ex-dividend inside any area pattern, lower the bounding lines by the
  amount of the dividend.

**Triangle measuring formula** — from the top of the first rally that began the pattern (its upper-left corner), draw a
line *parallel to the bottom boundary*; prices may be expected to reach it, climbing at roughly the same angle as the
pre-pattern trend (which dates the objective as well as prices it). Mirror it downward for declines. Less definite and
less reliable than the H&S formula, and weakened if the breakout is delayed into the apex.

- On weekly charts triangles are dependable; the "great, loose convergences" on monthly charts "had better be dismissed
  as without useful significance."
- Flags, Pennants and Wedges are also bounded by converging lines but are treated separately; diverging-boundary
  patterns are renamed **Broadening Formations** (Ch 10), not "inverted triangles."
- Closing caution: the beginner is proverbially lucky, then meets the exception. "Technical analysis of market action is
  not an exact science and never will be."

## Chapter 9 — Important Reversal Patterns, Continued: Rectangles, Double and Triple Tops (pp. 129–150)

**Rectangle** — a horizontal trading area bounded top and bottom by horizontal lines. If the symmetrical triangle is a
"picture of doubt," the rectangle is "a picture of conflict": two groups of roughly equal strength, one distributing at
the top (Supply Line), one accumulating at the bottom (Demand Line), batting the ball back and forth until one is
exhausted. Scalpers riding the range accentuate it.

Compared with the symmetrical triangle:

| Feature | Rectangle behaviour |
|---|---|
| Volume | Same — gradually diminishing; contrary action is suspect |
| Breakouts | Same rules (3%, volume on upside only) |
| False moves | **Much less frequent** — "almost as reliable as a Head-and-Shoulders," though less powerful |
| Premature breakouts | Slightly *more* frequent |
| Pullbacks/Throwbacks | More frequent — ~**40%** of cases, within 3 days to 3 weeks |
| Direction | Consolidation ~3 in 4; as a reversal, more often at **bottoms** than tops |
| Measuring | **Minimum = the width (height) of the rectangle**, projected from the breakout |

- Brief, wide, active, near-square rectangles are more dynamic than long narrow ones; moves out of the latter almost
  always hesitate at the minimum objective.
- Long, thin, dull rectangles are common at primary bottoms, grading into flat saucers/dormancy.
- Historical note: rectangles were far more common in the 1920s because they were manufactured by pool operations
  (planted rumours, wash sales) that the SEC subsequently outlawed.
- The rectangle is the individual-stock analogue of the **Dow Line**, but true rectangles with sharply delimited
  boundaries occur only in individual issues — an average's "line" is the algebraic sum of divergent individual charts.

**Double Tops and Bottoms** — "True Double Tops and Double Bottoms are exceedingly rare; Triple Forms are even rarer,"
and they can seldom be identified until long after the fact. The amateur over-reveres them.

- The problem: *every* ascending triangle and rectangle *begins* as two tops at the same level with less volume on the
  second. A criterion is needed to separate them.
- **Distinguishing criteria** — the two tops more than a **month** apart (most true ones 2–3 months or more), a long,
  dull, deep, rounding reaction between them (**≥20%**, sometimes as little as 15%), and less volume on the second
  advance. **Time matters more than depth**: the greater the separation, the less decline is needed.
- **Conclusive confirmation**: prices breaking *below the valley floor* between the tops. Then it is "usually a signal of
  major importance" — confirmed double tops seldom mark intermediate turns; they are a primary-reversal phenomenon.
- Measuring: no formula as such, but assume the decline carries at least as far below the valley as the peak-to-valley
  distance; it may take a series of waves.
- A **3% tolerance** applies to the two peaks' levels; curiously the second often exceeds the first fractionally. What
  matters is (1) buying cannot push into the clear decisively and (2) the support below is later broken.
- **Double Bottoms**: second bottom conspicuously dull and rounded (vs the spiky second *top*), volume rising on the
  rally out and markedly higher as the intervening high is surpassed. The second low is the market's "test" of support —
  but the test is not proven until prices exceed the intervening rally high on rising volume.
- **Triple Tops/Bottoms**: widely spaced, deep rounding reactions, volume less on each successive attempt. Need not be
  equally spaced; valleys need not be level; 3% tolerance applies. Confirmed by a break of the lower valley floor.
- "A Triple Bottom is always broken" is **not** a true saying — once confirmed by the required upside breakout it
  seldom fails. But an *uncompleted* triple bottom "must be regarded as treacherous."
- Nicknames: Double Tops → **"M" formations**; Triple Tops → **"W" patterns** (and the hybrid with a lower middle top
  resembles a W even more strikingly).
- Double/triple forms are easier to see on **weekly** charts; monthly charts show bottoms well but are too coarse for tops.
- Closing note on "chart sense": not genius, but "a skill that can be acquired by anyone of ordinary intelligence"
  through study and keeping score on yourself.

## Chapter 10 — Other Reversal Phenomena (pp. 151–180)

Opens with a scorecard of the eight reversal classes covered so far: (1) Head-and-Shoulders, (2) Multiple/Complex H&S,
(3) Rounding Turns, (4) Symmetrical Triangles, (5) Right-Angle Triangles, (6) Rectangles, (7) Double and Triple
Tops/Bottoms, (8) One-Day Reversal. **1, 2, 3, 7 are typically major turns; 4, 5, 6 more often intermediate. 1, 2, 3, 5
give directional warning before completion; 4 and 6 do not** and lean toward consolidation.

**Broadening Formations** — the diverging-boundary patterns (each minor top higher, each minor bottom lower).

- Characteristically **loose and irregular** (triangles are compact and regular), with reversals not landing cleanly on
  boundary lines.
- **Volume stays high and irregular throughout** — the opposite of a triangle. "The whole picture … is one of wild and
  apparently 'unintelligent' swings," suggesting a market out of control with the public excitedly committed.
- They appear almost exclusively in the **final phases of a long bull market** and are therefore **bearish nine times
  out of ten**. No new purchases; switch or cash out existing ones.
- Breakouts are hard to detect in real time. The usable tell is a **"failure"**: the last rally fails to exceed its
  predecessor (breaking the sequence) → expect a downside break; or the last reaction fails to undercut its predecessor
  → possible upside break. Confirmed only when the prior move in that direction is exceeded by ~3%.
- **Orthodox Broadening Top** — the strictly defined special case: three successively higher peaks with two intervening
  bottoms, the second lower than the first. Completed when the decline from the third peak breaks below the second
  bottom. It is a **"Five-Point Reversal"** (so is the H&S); indeed "a Broadening Top might be called a Head-and-Shoulders
  with a high right shoulder and a down-sloping neckline."
- A **pullback rally** follows in at least 4 of 5 cases, typically retracing about half (occasionally two-thirds) of the
  breakout move.
- Measuring implications are no greater than a large high-volume H&S.
- **There are no Broadening Bottoms** — the author never found a good one in thousands of charts. The conditions that
  create them don't exist after a prolonged decline, which corroborates the "excited public participation" reading.
- Reversal points in a true broadening top should be **no more than 2 months apart**; wide-spread broadening tendencies
  on monthly charts are not significant.
- **Right-Angled Broadening Formations** — one boundary horizontal. Generally bearish regardless of which side is flat,
  but the horizontal side means accumulation or distribution at a fixed price, so a decisive break *through it* has
  immediate force. A flat-topped version that breaks up decisively on volume (~3%) resumes the advance — rare but real.
  Flat-*bottomed* versions "almost always break down," usually with a pullback to the line. Watch the third reaction:
  if it carries below the second on undiminished volume, sell the next rally. If instead volume dries up and the third or
  fourth reaction holds above the previous low, the pattern becomes a **Head-and-Shoulders Consolidation** (Ch 11).
  Note the identity: "**every Head-and-Shoulders begins as a Broadening Formation.**"

**Diamond** — "a more or less Complex Head-and-Shoulders with a V-shaped neckline," or a broadening formation that
reverts into a symmetrical triangle. Not common; needs active markets, so it rarely appears at bottoms. Natural habitat:
major tops and high-volume tops preceding extensive intermediate reactions.

- **Do not force diamonds out of H&S patterns** just because a V-neckline signals earlier; require clean converging
  minor fluctuations and diminishing volume in the second half.
- **Measuring**: prices should move at least the greatest width of the pattern (head to the V of the neckline) from the
  breakout. A minimum; the trend usually carries well beyond.

**Wedge Formations** — converging boundaries that **both** slope the same way.

- **Rising Wedge** — both lines up, the lower steeper. Not bullish: unlike an ascending triangle there is no supply
  barrier to vault, only "a gradual petering out of investment interest." Each new wave is feebler. "A Rising Wedge
  typifies a situation which is growing progressively weaker in the technical sense," and its converging lines set a
  *limit* on the advance.
- Takes **more than 3 weeks** (shorter is a Pennant). Prices usually traverse ≥⅔ of the way to the apex, often to it,
  sometimes a last-gasp poke beyond. After the downside break, decline is prompt and usually retraces all the ground
  gained inside the wedge.
- **Falling Wedge** — identical in form, different in aftermath: prices tend to drift sideways or "saucer around" before
  rising. So a rising wedge demands quick action; a falling wedge allows the trader to take his time.
- Volume diminishes toward the apex as in a triangle.
- Both must be *well defined*: compact, frequent fluctuations, clearly converging, marked slope. If one line nears the
  horizontal, treat it as a right-angle triangle instead.
- Mostly too short-lived to show on monthly charts. **Beware a scaling artifact**: on arithmetic monthly charts a whole
  major bear decline can *look* like a giant falling wedge, because equal percentage swings shrink in points as prices
  fall. On semi-log the same move shows a parallel or widening channel.
- **Rising wedges are a characteristic pattern for bear-market rallies** — frequent wedges after an extensive decline
  are evidence the primary trend is still down. On an arithmetically scaled weekly chart a rising wedge "is almost
  invariably a Bear Market phenomenon."

**One-Day Reversal** — a day of unusually high volume (exceeding anything for months) after a long steady move. Prices
race ahead from the opening, often gapping; the tape runs late; a 2–3% intraday range is traversed; then the trend
reverses and the close comes back near where the day started, leaving a small net change.

- Carries only minor/temporary significance alone — it signals the turn of the "head," not the decline that follows.
- Common in thin stocks with an active advance and a large public following; rare in the averages. The *bottom* version
  is conspicuous in the averages.
- Its real value: it appears *within* or at the start of more pregnant formations and is "an urgent warning to watch
  closely the chart in which it has appeared." The apex shakeout of a symmetrical triangle often takes this form.

**Selling Climax** — the one-day reversal at the bottom of a panic, produced by distress/margin selling feeding on
itself until millions of shares are dumped in a final clean-out.

- It radically reverses the technical condition: shares pass from weak to strong hands at panic prices and the
  overhanging weight of potential selling is removed.
- Recognition in real time: a decline of panic proportions, a substantial **downside opening gap**, an unabsorbable flood
  of offerings, a late ticker, an exceptionally **broad** market — then, some time after 11 a.m., selling dries up, a few
  issues begin to climb, and suddenly prices are jumping. That is the moment to act. Buy something thoroughly depressed
  but always well-followed; **don't hang on too long**.
- Records cited: 29 Oct 1929 (DJIA opened 252.38, fell to 212.33 — 40.05 points — rallied to 230.07, 16,410,000 shares);
  19 Oct 1937 ("Black Tuesday", closed 126.85 recovering its entire loss on 7,290,000 shares). In both cases **the panic
  low was broken a few weeks later.**
- "Selling Climaxes do not normally occur at the final Bottoms of Bear Markets" — only the 1938–39 bear ended with its
  first panic phase.
- Bassetti adds 1987: DJIA 2746.65 (25 Aug) → 1616.21 (20 Oct), a 1,130-point / 41% retracement, with 19–20 October
  traversing 547.95 points (25% of the market). *"Plus ça change…"* and "readers should not assume that similar crashes
  will not occur in the future."
- The **Island Reversal** is deferred to Chapter 12.

## Chapter 10.1 — Short-Term Phenomena of Potential Importance (pp. 181–188) · *Bassetti*

Adds three modern one-day/few-day patterns to the classical set, for scalpers and speculators — "but it does not hurt
the long-term investor to know and understand them."

- **Spike** — a day protruding head and shoulders above (or plunging far below) the days *before and after*; therefore
  not identifiable on the day itself. Importance is judged by (A) the strength and length of the preceding move,
  (B) where it closes (down at a top, up at a bottom), (C) its prominence vs neighbouring days. A church-spire top.
- **Runaway Day / Wide-Ranging Day** — an unusually long range, often opening at the low and closing at the high (or the
  reverse), traversing two to three times the normal daily range. The *following* days reveal its significance:
  consolidation on continued volume confirms it; tapering volume or a rounding/volatile pullback calls it into question.
- **Key Reversal Day** — a new high in an up-move followed by a **close below the previous day's close**. Tactically: in
  a major top, short on the close with a stop at or just above the day's high; exit on an opposite key reversal, a profit
  target, or a chart pattern — or treat it as the first unit of a position for an anticipated bear market. Also useful
  in trading-range markets.
- **The unifying rule for all very short-term patterns** (gaps, one-day reversals, key reversal days, spikes, runaway
  days): *"return of prices to the origination of the formation marks the formation as a false signal and reason to
  reverse the trade direction"* — often for significant profit.
- "When trendlines go vertical, blow-off management must be used."

## Chapter 11 — Consolidation Formations (pp. 189–210)

The military analogy: an army that has outrun its supplies must halt, dig in and build a base before attacking again.
Almost any sideways congestion functions as consolidation **provided volume diminishes during its construction and it
shows no broadening tendency**.

**Flags and Pennants** — the signature consolidations of fast moves.

- **Flag**: a small compact parallelogram (a tilted rectangle) sloping *against* the prevailing trend, forming after a
  near-vertical "mast." Successive minor tops and bottoms each slightly lower (in an uptrend), volume shrinking markedly
  and constantly, then an eruption that "practically duplicates the original mast."
- Very short, "solid" flags may be horizontal or even slope slightly *with* the trend. Down-trend flags slope up.
- **Pennant**: identical except bounded by *converging* lines — a small compact sloping triangle, or a short compact
  wedge. Volume dries up even faster than in a flag, sometimes to almost nothing.
- **The measuring formula ("the Flag flies at half-mast")**: measure from the point where the preceding move broke away
  from a previous pattern/trendline/resistance (recognisable by a spurt in volume) to the level where the flag began;
  project the same distance from the flag's breakout point. Best applied as **chart distance on semi-log paper**, since
  advances tend to exceed and declines to fall short of the arithmetic count.
- **Three reliability tests** — (1) it must follow a straight-line move; (2) activity must diminish appreciably and
  *continuously* until the breakaway; (3) prices must break out in the expected direction **within 4 weeks**; anything
  beyond **3 weeks** is suspect. High or irregular volume inside the pattern turns it into a minor *reversal* area.
- "Among the most dependable of chart formations, both as to directional and measuring indications" — and they almost
  never fail without warning first.
- Where they appear: **the later, dynamic phase of bull markets** (so their appearance warns the advance is in its final
  weeks), and the second (panic) stage of bear markets, where they complete in 3–4 days rather than weeks. Downtrend
  flags after a major decline "must be regarded with caution."
- A true flag **cannot** appear on a monthly chart and barely shows on a weekly; a flag-*shaped* long-range area is
  frequently a major reversal area in disguise. Check the dailies.

**Other consolidations**

- **Rectangular Consolidations** are an *early*-phase phenomenon (contrast flags/pennants), appearing in the early stages
  of bull trends, and in bear markets either just before a panic decline or in the last stage (premature accumulation).
- **Head-and-Shoulders Consolidation** — an *inverted* H&S relative to the prevailing trend: an uptrend produces an H&S
  *bottom* shape, a downtrend an H&S *top* shape. No confusion is possible once complete, but the head stage is
  ambiguous. **Volume diminishes on all three parts** instead of following the reversal rule; breakouts behave normally.
  The H&S measuring formula does **not** work reliably here — these patterns are usually flat and the move typically
  runs well beyond (sometimes short of) the measurement. Complex/multiple versions are very rare.
- **Scallops / Repeated Saucers** — in large, actively traded issues emerging from a long-time bottom, a major advance
  often proceeds as a series of slightly up-tilted saucers. Typical dimensions: each scallop nets about **10–15%**, the
  reaction from left lip to bottom runs **20–30%**, and the duration is **5–7 weeks** (rarely under 3). "Three steps up
  for every two back." Buy at the dull rounding bottom of each scallop — exactly where most tape-watchers lose interest,
  while they buy the "new high on volume" at the top. Above about 15 the pattern gets irregular; above 20 it usually
  gives way to straight-line advances with standard consolidations.
- **Modern vs old-style markets**: SEC regulation, higher margins and greater sophistication made rectangles and
  right-angle triangles less common and symmetrical triangles looser, while flags, pennants and "normal" trend patterns
  (H&S, rounding turns) are as common as ever. "The chart student of 1907 would be quite at home with the charts of
  1966" — and, Bassetti adds, with those of 2000. His 9th-edition note: pools and manipulators are replaced by new forms
  of skullduggery; patterns are less neat, horizontal lines behave more like *zones* than lines, and more judgment is
  required — "but everything which Edwards says here might have been written in 2005."

## Chapter 12 — Gaps (pp. 211–230)

A gap is "a price range at which (at the time it occurred) no shares changed hands." The chapter's main work is
demolition: the superstition that **"a gap must be closed."**

- A gap is "closed"/"covered" when a later trend retraces its range. Will it be closed eventually? Probably — but
  possibly years later, which is "hardly a matter of interest to the ordinary trader." The investor who bought C&O at
  260 in October 1929 counting on a 2-point gap closing waited nearly 7 years to get out even, watching his investment
  shrink to a sixth in between. Hundreds of 1929–30 gaps have never been closed.
- The probability of a gap being closed is no different from the probability of a stock returning to *any* price it has
  traded at.
- **Thousands of intraday gaps exist and never appear on daily charts** — "the gap theorists are oblivious of them,
  although their significance is often greater." Practically every emphatic breakout from a rectangle or right-angle
  triangle involves a gap; only those at the opening bell show up.

**Gaps with no forecasting value** (eliminate these first): one-eighth gaps and other normal bid–ask spreads; habitual
gaps in thin medium/high-priced issues ("if your chart shows numerous gaps as a regular thing, then no one of them is
apt to mean anything"); and **ex-dividend gaps**, which reflect a change in book value, not in supply and demand.

**The four significant classes**

1. **Common / Area / Pattern Gap** — occurs *inside* a congestion area, where activity concentrates at the supply and
   demand lines and the middle is "no-man's land." Usually closed within days. Forecasting significance "practically
   nil," but useful: their appearance implies an area pattern is forming and reinforces the expectation that it will be
   a **consolidation** rather than a reversal. A gap on the last traverse before a breakout, however, is not closed soon
   and has no reason to be.
2. **Breakaway Gap** — occurs on the move that *completes* a formation and breaks prices away. Most breakouts through a
   horizontal boundary produce one, because a "vacuum" of offerings exists just above a flat-topped pattern. Value:
   (a) it confirms the breakout is genuine — "false moves are seldom attended by gaps"; (b) it implies more force behind
   the move. No measuring implication. **Will it be closed?** Read the volume: heavy volume at the takeoff level but
   little beyond the gap → roughly 50–50 that the next minor reaction fills it. Heavy volume *beyond* the gap → a
   throwback is almost always stopped at its outer edge. Lacking a clue, assume it won't be filled until long after the
   pattern's move has played out.
3. **Continuation / Runaway / "Measuring" Gap** — the technically richest. Not associated with area patterns; it occurs
   in the middle of a rapid straight-line move, at the stage where prices move most easily relative to transactions —
   **approximately the halfway point**. *Inference: prices will travel as far beyond the gap as they already have from
   the start of the move to the gap*, measured vertically on the chart. Works particularly well on semi-log; on
   arithmetic, expect a little more upside and a little less downside, and bank on something short of the goal. With two
   or three such gaps, "average the picture mentally" — look for the centre of thinness — and lean conservative, because
   each successive gap brings the move nearer exhaustion. **"The measuring-gap rule should be used for purposes of
   'getting out' rather than 'getting in.'"**
4. **Exhaustion Gap** — a wide gap at the very *end* of a move that never lost momentum but hit a wall. The test comes
   on the day *after*: extraordinary volume with price failing to advance commensurately → exhaustion; a **Reversal Day**
   closing back near the gap's edge makes it "a virtual certainty." Supporting evidence: the pattern of origin has
   already fulfilled its measurement (exhaustion likely) or is still far short (continuation likely). **An exhaustion
   gap is seldom the first gap in a runaway move** — assume the first is a continuation gap and treat each successive
   one with more suspicion, especially if wider. Exhaustion gaps are **quickly closed, usually within 2–5 days** — which
   is the final diagnostic, and which neatly inverts the superstition: the runaway gap is *not* closed and the trend runs
   on; the exhaustion gap *is* closed, and the closing is part of the signal. By itself it says "stop," not "reverse" —
   but it warrants closing out commitments at once.

**Island Reversal** — a compact trading range isolated by an **exhaustion gap** on the way in and a **breakaway gap** on
the way out, the two gaps overlapping at about the same level so the area stands alone on the chart.

- May be a single day (then usually a one-day reversal) or up to a week of minor fluctuations; characterised by
  relatively high volume.
- Not a major reversal formation in itself, but "it does, as a rule, send prices back for a complete retracement of the
  Minor Move which preceded it" — and islands frequently appear *inside* larger patterns at primary turning points
  (e.g. in the head of a dynamic H&S top).
- Why gaps can repeat at the same level becomes clear in Ch 13: prices move most easily through ranges where little
  stock changed hands and previous owners have no "vested interest."
- Hard to trade on except as a scalp, since much of the retracement is done by the time it is visible. Its best use is
  "calling attention to a situation."
- **Gaps in the averages**: less frequent (a majority of components must gap simultaneously). Pattern gaps are rare;
  breakaway and runaway types are not uncommon but small; exhaustion gaps and islands are rare. Interpretation is the
  same. The broadest indexes show the fewest and smallest gaps — though Bassetti notes the NASDAQ "is quite volatile and
  a good gap producer."

## Chapter 13 — Support and Resistance (pp. 231–252)

"Some experienced traders have built their 'systems' almost entirely on the principles of what we here call Support and
Resistance, paying no attention to the specific pictorial patterns."

**Definitions.** **Support** = buying, actual or potential, sufficient in volume to halt a downtrend for an appreciable
period. **Resistance** = selling sufficient to satisfy all bids and stop an advance. A Support *Range* represents a
concentration of demand; a Resistance *Range*, a concentration of supply.

**The central mechanism.** Turnover concentrates at the price levels where a great many shares changed hands before, and
because high-volume levels become reversal points, **reversal levels tend to repeat**. The non-obvious half, which
"many casual chart observers appear never to grasp":

> **These critical levels constantly switch their roles. A former Top, once surpassed, becomes a Bottom zone in a
> subsequent downtrend; an old Bottom, once penetrated, becomes a Top zone in a later advance.**

- The psychology: buyers who bought at 50 thinking it cheap, saw it rally to 60, then watched it break to 45, now say
  "I'll just wait until it gets back up to 50 where I can get out even." Mirror image for sold-out bulls who want their
  stock back at the price they sold.
- Only a *moderate* oversupply is needed to stifle an advance — the balance is delicate, and other traders watching the
  tape will join the parade, so orders for a few hundred shares can induce the transfer of thousands.
- Those who created the level are "among the wiser and more alert" — so in a marked bear trend overhanging supply
  appears *below* the theoretical resistance level (wise owners sacrifice a point to get out), and in an established
  bull market reactions stop at the *uppermost* limits of support while bear-market recoveries reach only the *lowest*
  edges of resistance, or fail of it.

**Three criteria for estimating potential**

1. **Volume** — how many shares changed hands there. A single sharp high-volume bottom offers somewhat more resistance
   than a series of bottoms with the same total volume spread over time with intervening rallies.
2. **Distance away** — how far prices fell below the level (prices must fall far enough to convince the buyer he made a
   bad investment). Rough rule: don't expect much supply from a low-middle-priced (20–35) bottom unless the trend later
   took quotations **more than 10% under it**. The rule does not apply to very low-priced issues. A deeper decline also
   adds *new* owners with large profits who will sell at the old level.
3. **Time elapsed** (and intervening market history) — a supply one or two years old is more effective than one four or
   five years old, "but the latter does not lose all of its potency by any means." Split-ups, large stock dividends and
   unusually generous cash distributions blur the owner's memory of his cost and abate his desire to get out even.

Plus: **once a resistance zone has been attacked and repelled an advance, some of its power is consumed** — "it is an
odds-on assumption that a third attack at a Resistance Level will succeed in penetrating it."

- Judgment is unavoidable; the great danger is **underestimating** resistance. "It is safer always to overestimate it."
- **Locating the precise level**: inspect the price and volume pattern of the old congestion and mentally average the
  *closing* levels of its days — that figure approximates the zone's "centre of gravity."
- Chart choice: dailies for minor levels; a **weekly chart with volume covering at least a full bull-and-bear cycle** is
  best for major and intermediate zones (a daily chart over-weights recent minor levels); monthly charts also work well.
- **"Normal"/ideal trend** = a self-correcting zigzag where each reaction returns to the approximate top of the previous
  advance (10→6→15→10→20→15→26→20…). Technicians regard this as particularly sound, and the return to support is a good
  place to buy.
- **Significance of support failure**: a reaction that sifts *through* the previous minor top carries "a distinct
  warning of a change in trend," emphatically so if volume picks up. Note *change*, not necessarily reversal — it may
  mean only sideways consolidation. "The breaking of a Minor Support should always be regarded as the first step in the
  Reversal of the Intermediate Trend"; breaking an *intermediate* support is frequently the first sign of a **major**
  reversal. (The decline from the head of an H&S top is precisely such a break.)
- **A popular misconception corrected**: boardroom usage calls the last reaction low the support. If X rose to 62,
  reacted to 57, then pushed to 68, most traders call 57 the support — the authors name **62**. There is no vested
  interest at 57; there *is* at 62. "It is much easier for prices to push up through a former Top level than through the
  Resistance set up at a previous volume Bottom."
- **Round Figures** (20, 30, 50, 75, 100) produce support/resistance without any vested interest, because they become
  mental profit objectives — especially in new all-time high ground. They matter less in old, actively traded issues.
- **Repeating historical levels** — in old-line stocks the same zones recur across cycles for decades (General Electric
  22–24, 34–35, 40–42, 48–50; Southern Pacific 21–22, 28–30, 38–40, 55–56; U.S. Steel 42–45, 55–58, 69–72, 78–80, 93–96).
  Levels gradually blur and new ones are created.
- **Bear-market panics are the great exception**: they "pay no heed whatever to previous underlying Support Zones" and
  sweep them away until a selling climax. But a high-turnover **panic bottom** acquires a strong vested interest and
  furnishes conspicuous resistance later.
- **Pattern resistance** — the mechanism explains the patterns: a broken descending-triangle demand line becomes
  resistance; a broken H&S neckline becomes resistance; a rectangle's supply line becomes support once decisively
  exceeded. Throwbacks to a pattern boundary need no 10%-away rule — a conclusive breakout is enough.
- **The Symmetrical Triangle's support "field"** is different: the high-volume **first reversal point** becomes the
  support if the breakout carries clearly beyond it; otherwise a throwback goes to the extended sloping boundary; and if
  the reaction comes after the apex is passed in time, support is at the **apex level**. The apex (the "cradle") is the
  axis of the triangle's support/resistance, strongest near the cradle point and weaker as it extends to the right. If
  axis support fails, expect an **"end run around the line"** — place a stop under the apex level.
- **Volume on breaks through support** — when prices hold at a level for several days and then push through, there is
  nearly always a sudden acceleration and marked volume pickup *after* the penetration. Confirmatory. The authors
  decline to explain the cause: "causes for many technical phenomena, such as this one, may be left to the academicians,
  provided the practical implications are clear."
- **In the averages**: zones cannot be construed as narrowly; minor tops and bottoms are less dependable, but clearly
  defined intermediate reversals produce strong levels. A stock holding its individual support while the averages break
  theirs is *presumably* better placed for the next recovery — "other things being equal," and relative-performance
  claims are only one minor factor in the overall picture.

## Chapter 14 — Trendlines and Channels (pp. 253–280)

"This phenomenon is, in truth, the most fascinating, impressive, and mysterious of all that the stock charts exhibit" —
that minor, intermediate and even multi-year primary swings trace what look like ruled straight lines.

**Construction.** In an **uptrend** the genuinely straight line connects the **lower extremes of the minor recessions**
(the tops are less even). In a **downtrend** it connects the **tops of the minor rallies**. These are the **Basic
Trendlines**. Two established reversal points are required to fix one — boundary lines of triangles and rectangles, and
H&S necklines, are simply special types of trendline. **Never draw a line from a bottom to a top, or through the price
track.**

**Two functions**: (1) a decisive break signals the move has run out and calls time for the intermediate trader; (2) a
small top reversal forming well *above* an intact trendline can be deliberately ignored — "he can hold on so long as
the trendline holds," which protects against the expensive habit of switching out on every minor setback.

**Three tests of a trendline's authority**

1. **Number of contacts** — the more bottoms formed at/near the line, the greater its significance. Each "test"
   increases it. Weighted *more heavily* than length.
2. **Length / duration** — but a line drawn from two bottoms less than a week apart is error-prone (too steep or, more
   often, too flat). Points far enough apart to be independent wave components make a truer line.
3. **Angle** — "the flatter, more nearly horizontal the trendline, the more important it is technically." A very steep
   line can be broken by a brief sideways consolidation and is of little forecasting value. On TEKNIPLAT semi-log paper,
   intermediate daily uptrends in stocks priced 10–50 rise at roughly **30°** — thin speculative issues steeper, heavy
   investment stocks flatter.

**Three tests of a valid penetration**

1. **Extent** — a close beyond the line by about **3%** of the stock's price (2% in the averages). It need not happen in
   one day.
2. **Volume** — not required for a downside break (an up-trendline break is analogous to a downside pattern breakout),
   but in practice an intermediate up-trendline break is "much more often than not" attended by visibly increased
   activity, which is useful in borderline cases (e.g. a 2% close plus conspicuous volume at the day's low can be read
   as decisive).
3. **The pullback test** — a borderline break followed by a feeble, volume-less rally that rounds over under the line
   without closing above it makes the situation critical; the slightest renewed selling confirms it.
   **Beware the shakeout**: a high-volume crack through the line that closes back above it is usually a false move for
   that moment — but watch the next few days very closely, "else a shakeout could not have been easily staged."

**Amendment rules.** If the original line rested on only two points and the third contact penetrates it indecisively,
redraw across the first and third (or second and third) bottoms. If the line has already been *confirmed* by a third or
fourth bottom, disregard the indecisive break. Intraday breaks that don't close beyond the line are ignored — closing
prices often make a better trendline than extreme lows, especially in thin erratic stocks. "A bit of experimenting with
different lines often pays. A thin, transparent ruler is especially useful."

**Double Trendlines / Trend Ranges** — two parallel lines a point or so apart often define the trend better than any
single line: sharp shakeout bottoms fall on the outer/lower line, duller rounded reactions stop at the inner/upper one.
"Really plentiful, although the majority of chart technicians seem to be quite unaware of them." The trend isn't over
until the **outer** line is decisively penetrated. Warning sign: prices reaching the outer line *steadily* rather than by
a quick shake, then struggling to rally back through the inner line.

**Trend Channels** — the **Return Line** is the parallel across the opposite extremes; the area between is the channel.
Best defined in actively traded large issues.

- Its greatest value is **negative**, not as a profit-taking target: once a channel is established, **a rally that fails
  to reach the Return Line signals deterioration**.
- Two quantitative corollaries: *the margin by which a rally fails to reach the Return Line frequently equals the margin
  by which the ensuing decline penetrates the Basic Trendline*; and *when a reaction bottoms above the Basic Trendline,
  the next advance usually pushes out through the Return Line by about the same margin*.
- **Experimental lines** — the practice of drawing every trendline the action permits, lightly, erasing the useless ones
  and heavying the ones that stand up, is "the quickest way — in fact, the only way — of acquiring the experience."

**Consequences of penetration — the Throwback.** After a decisive break of an intermediate up-trendline, expect either
a full intermediate recession or a consolidation area. And expect a **pullback to the projected trendline** — one of the
book's open mysteries: pullbacks follow trendline breaks *more* frequently than pattern breaks and stop *more exactly*
at the old line, at a level determined by two variables (the line's slope and the time it is reached). "No one knows
why." Note the asymmetry: a Return Line does **not** function as support after prices break out above it.

**Intermediate downtrends** are "far less regular and uniform": steeper, and prices tend to curve *away* from (accelerate
below) any line drawn across the first two rally tops. Return lines and good channels are rare in declines. But keep
drawing the lines anyway — prices tend to thrash out a base sideways, so the trend often doesn't visibly turn up until
the down-trendline is finally reached and broken on the upside.

- **An important clue to a bear market's end**: the *last* intermediate downswing of a major bear market is usually
  cleaner, more orderly and less precipitous — a normal-looking trend that merely slants down. If such a decline holds a
  steady not-too-steep course, is contacted several times by minor rallies, produces a consistent channel, and prices do
  not fall out through the Return Line, "the eventual upside penetration of this trendline may well signal a Major Turn."

**Corrective trends and the Fan Principle.** Intermediate counter-trend moves take three forms: (a) a consolidation
pattern with little net retracement (no trendline needed — the commonest); (b) an orderly straight-line retracement of
moderate slope to the nearest support, retracing a third to a half (relatively rare, but produces good trendlines); and
(c) the **fan**, nearly as common as (a).

> **The Three-Fan Rule.** A sharp initial reaction produces a steep minor trendline; a quick rally breaks it; a duller,
> flatter decline follows, producing a second line drawn from the original high across the top of the first upthrust;
> that breaks, and a third, still flatter sell-off produces a third line. **When the third fan line is broken, the low
> of the intermediate correction has been seen.** The whole move has a "saucering-out" shape.

- Prices consistently throw back to the preceding fan line after each upthrust, and the new primary swing starts slowly,
  carrying out the saucer picture.
- Exceptions exist, but the rule works in the great majority of cases — and it offers an entry with a logically very
  near stop.
- **Applies only to corrective moves** — intermediate reactions in a bull market, intermediate recoveries in a bear.
- Closing note: "Technical analysis of a stock chart is something like putting together a jigsaw puzzle" — volume,
  pattern, measurement, support/resistance, trendlines, general market, all fitted into place.

## Chapter 15 — Major Trendlines (pp. 281–294)

Here the arithmetic-vs-logarithmic choice becomes material, and the answer is **stock-specific**.

- On **arithmetic** monthly charts, bull trends in actively traded speculative commons tend to **accelerate** — curving
  away from any straight line drawn through early bottoms, so the line isn't touched again until long after the top.
  Many of these produce straight trends on **log** scale, where the line breaks sooner and at a higher price: log gives
  the better signal.
- But substantial investment/semi-investment issues (Consolidated Edison, General Motors, Libbey-Owens-Ford) advance in
  straight **arithmetic** trends; high-grade preferreds *decelerate* even on arithmetic; and a fourth class curves up on
  arithmetic *and* rolls over on log.
- The resolution: **study the individual issue's history.** "Stocks — most of them, at least — do not change their habits
  and their technical characteristics much from one Bull and Bear cycle to the next." Over decades a speculative issue
  may acquire an investment rating, and its trend type will migrate accordingly — but major patterns repeat.
- **Construction tip**: the most useful major bull trendline is drawn **not from the absolute bear-market low but from
  the next intermediate bottom** — the accumulation area is long and flat, so the first line is too near horizontal to
  express the genuine markup trend. The same applies to intermediate moves out of area formations: *"the true
  Intermediate Trendline usually starts from the right shoulder rather than from the head."*
- Tests of significance are as for intermediate lines, with a little more leeway on penetrations; what monthly charts are
  for is **perspective**.
- **Major downtrends are nearly useless.** Bear markets are steeper (they last about half as long) and *accelerating*,
  which semi-log plotting accentuates. 1929–32 was "the only one in all stock market records which produced a
  Straight-Line Major Downtrend" — and the beautiful lines of that period led chart students to expect the same in every
  bear market. Don't expect major trendlines to call a primary bottom; the clue is the final intermediate downswing's
  trendline (Ch 14).
- **Major channels** also fail: swings widen in points as a bull market works upward, so the return line diverges rather
  than parallels. Semi-log corrects this for bull trends but then the opposite problem appears in bear markets, which
  neither scaling compensates for.
- **In the averages**, everything works better: broad indexes "produce more regular trends and, in consequence, more
  exactly applicable trendlines," because averaging smooths component vagaries. Therefore construe them more tightly —
  **2% penetration is ample in the averages** vs 3% in an individual stock. "It is still easier to swim with the tide
  than against it."
- Bassetti's update: index proliferation means the Dow alone is now questionable as a read on the broad trend; use the
  **Dow Industrials, S&P 500 and NASDAQ Composite**.

## Chapter 15.1 — Trading the Averages in the 21st Century (pp. 295–300) · *Bassetti*

Modern instruments make Magee's observation — that the averages are technically smoother than individual issues —
directly tradable. The chapter tests Magee's **2% trendline-break filter** against the 1987 crash, the 1990s top and
the 2000–2002 decline, showing the same signals in real time and in hindsight.

- Across the 1929, 1987 and 1998–2000 tops, "in each case the crash occurred after the nearest important trendline had
  been decisively broken — usually trendlines of approximately 3 months by 2% or more, and sometimes accompanied by
  reversal formations."
- "All historic tops will show evidence of attempts to resume the trend after a break of this kind. Belief dies hard.
  Nonetheless, hedging or exiting on these trend breaks proves to be the best strategy over and over again."
- **Important qualification (9th ed.)**: "the breaking of a trendline is as much a *warning* as a signal to act. The
  break, instead of a change of trend to the reverse, may indicate a change of trend to the **sideways** — into a
  reversal or continuation pattern."
- Bassetti's reading of the 1999–2000 Dow top: a broadening top resolving into a diamond, then triangles, all of which
  could be read as "a gnarly messy rectangle" — and "it is not so important to identify triangles in this situation as
  to recognize the general nature of the market — sideways to down."
- "A little old lady with a ruler could have saved her portfolio here."

## Chapter 16 — Technical Analysis of Commodity Charts (pp. 301–306)

Patterns should appear wherever price is set by free supply and demand, and they do: "speculative aims and speculators'
psychology are the same whether the goods dealt in be corporate shares or contracts for the future delivery of cotton
bales." But four intrinsic differences change the weighting.

1. **Limited life** — a futures contract trades ~18 months then vanishes, so **long-term support and resistance levels
   have no meaning whatever.**
2. **Hedging** — as much as 80% of volume is commercial hedging, not speculation, so even near-term support/resistance is
   less potent, and seasonal factors must be weighed (including the meaning of their absence).
3. **Volume** — there is no fixed "shares outstanding"; open interest may exceed potential supply many times over,
   legitimately. Volume interpretation is correspondingly complicated.
4. **Weather** — droughts and floods can change the trend immediately, drastically and unforeseeably.

**What transfers well vs badly**

| Works well in commodities | Works less well |
|---|---|
| Head-and-Shoulders formations | Triangles, Rectangles, Flags (less frequent, less reliable) |
| Rounding Tops and Bottoms | Support and Resistance (sometimes perfect, as often not) |
| **Basic trendlines — "better defined and more useful in commodities than in stocks"** | Gaps (relatively less significance) |

- Historical arc: technical analysis of commodity charts worked until ~1941–42, was destroyed by wartime government
  intervention ("fortunes were made and lost … but they were not made from the charts"), and by 1956 worked again under
  "more orderly" regulation that left the evaluative function intact.
- Strong warning: commodity speculation "requires far more specialized knowledge, demands more constant daily and
  hourly attention … he might better shun commodity speculation entirely unless he is prepared to make a career of it."
- Bassetti (a former registered Commodity Trading Advisor) agrees futures are "eminently tradable" with these methods
  but repeats that the tempo, leverage and character are so different that "the novice risks life, limb, and capital in
  entering the area unescorted."

## Chapter 16.1 — Technical Analysis of Commodity Charts, Part 2: A 21st Century Perspective (pp. 307–318) · *Bassetti*

A history of mechanical futures trading and an argument for chart analysis as its necessary superstructure.

- **The California systems traders (1970s)** — NASA "rocket scientists" building computerised systems that returned
  ~100% a year while the Dow ground around 1000. "Then the markets changed. Mechanical systems which cut through the
  markets like a reaper in a wheat field in bull markets grind up capital like sausage in sideways markets."
- **The Turtles (1980s)** — Richard Dennis's trainees, reportedly ~80% a year; "in the late '90s the Turtles were made
  into turtle soup." The Turtle system is "basically an adaptation of **Richard Donchian's channel breakout system**"
  (long on a 20-day high, short on a 20-day low), which Dunn & Hargitt rated best of the systems they tested.
- **The moral, stated twice**: "Science and genius are … the happy combination of man, method, moment, and market."
- **The core argument**: mechanical systems "are blind. All they see are ones and zeros." They cannot tell a *trending*
  market from a *trading* market. The experienced chart analyst can — "this market has not yet made a bottom and the time
  to begin trading it long has not yet come" — and can adjust tactics accordingly. "The addition to these systems of
  prudently applied chart analysis will immeasurably improve their performance and risk characteristics."
- Chart analysis is a **"natural" system** (so are Dow Theory and the Turtle high/low system): "no mathematical
  algorithm comes between the analyst and the data." Its weakness/strength is that it is qualitative and cannot be
  mechanised — which is also why counter-strategies cannot easily be built against it.
- Warning on natural systems too: "when the markets learn that a lot of capital is waiting just above the 20 day high
  they will set a trap for it."
- **Stops**: technical stops (basing points, support/resistance) are preferred to money-management stops, but "if some
  logical and disciplined method of setting and observing stops is not installed the trader is assured of failure."
  O'Neil's 8%-under-entry rule is "a little crude … but better than no risk calculation at all"; use the Scott procedure
  (Ch 26) to size the position. "Numerous studies have proven that the size of the risk per trade — 1%, 2%, 3% — is
  directly correlated to equity volatility."
- "What they mean by discipline is their ability to measure and contain the risk, set a stop based on technical or money
  management procedures and then **honor the stop**."
- 2005–06 charts shown: rounding bottoms in **gold** (1997–2004) and **silver** (1998–2005), a long-term **double bottom
  in the CRB index**, and tight-trendline-plus-run-day tactics in silver and T-bonds — with the forecast that "the next
  great bull market in commodities … may be about to occur."

## Chapter 17 — A Summary and Some Concluding Comments (pp. 319–324)

The methodical recap. Charts are "only the rather imperfect instruments by which we hope to gauge the relative strength
of supply and demand." Ask constantly: *"What does this action really mean in terms of supply and demand?"*

**The four categories of method**

1. **Area patterns** (with volume) — indicate an important change in the supply–demand balance; signal consolidation or
   reversal; some provide measurements. These furnish "most of his 'get in' and many of his 'get out' signals."
   On volume: it is relative; it runs higher near a bull top than a bear bottom; it follows the trend; a notable increase
   may mean *breakout*, *climax* or (more rarely) *shakeout* — "its meaning, in any given case, can be determined by its
   relation to the price pattern."
2. **Trend and trendline studies** — supplement area patterns for direction and change of direction; usable for get-in
   and get-out in short-term trading; above all "they provide a defense against premature relinquishment of profitable
   long-term positions."
3. **Support and Resistance levels** — created by other people's existing commitments. They can show where to take a
   position, but their more important function is to show where a move will slow or end. "Before entering a trade, look
   both to the pattern of origin for an indication of the power behind the move, and to the history of Support–Resistance
   for an indication as to whether it can proceed without difficulty for a profitable distance." Especially useful for
   cash-in and switch signals.
4. **Broad market background, including Dow Theory** — late, flawed, and still "an invaluable adjunct to the technical
   trader's Toolkit." Keep the characteristic phases of the primary cycle in view.

**The closing admonitions**

- *"More money has been lost by buying perfectly good stocks in the later and most exciting phases of a Bull Market, and
  then selling them, perhaps from necessity, in the discouraging conditions prevailing in a Bear Market, than from all
  other causes combined!"*
- "It may pay to buck 'the public,' but it does not ever pay to buck the real underlying trend."
- On the "always late" criticism: partly true. **"But the secret of success lies not in buying at the very lowest
  possible price and selling at the absolute top. It is in the avoidance of large losses."** One of Wall Street's most
  successful operators (Bassetti: Bernard Baruch) never bought within 5 points of a bottom or sold within 5 of a top.
- "There is nothing in the science of technical analysis that requires one always to have a position in the market…
  Patience is as much a virtue in stock trading as in any other human activity."

## Chapter 17.1 — Technical Analysis and Technology in the 21st Century (pp. 325–332) · *Bassetti*

Puts the computer and the Internet in perspective. Magee's Springfield chart room — a room of chartists from teenagers
to senior citizens who hand-drew the charts in this book — has been replaced by "a beige (sometimes lime) box which
sits crowded on our desktops and which is often worshipped as a fount of insight and wisdom."

- "More money has been spent in this effort than was ever put into the search for the philosopher's stone." No fail-safe
  algorithm has been found; the research has shown that even "buy low sell high" has fatal flaws.
- **The schools**: chart (Magee-type) analysts; point-and-figure chartists; candlestick chartists; and **number-driven /
  statistical** technicians (moving averages, %R, Bollinger Bands…). All use hard data — "data which people cannot lie
  about, misrepresent, and manipulate, unlike the data which are input to fundamental analysis."
- **The key asymmetry**: stock markets are **behavioural** (driven by human emotion, unquantified); options markets are
  **model-driven**, and there quantitative analysis genuinely works. "Alas, life is not so simple for the simple stock
  trader."
- **A striking claim**: "The fact that chart analysis is not mechanizable is important. It is one reason chart analysis
  continues to be effective in the hands of a skilled practitioner. Not being susceptible to mechanization,
  counter-strategies cannot be brought against it" — except where the level is obvious to everyone, in which case
  participants will deliberately push prices through it, creating bull and bear traps. **"Interestingly, the failure of
  these signals may constitute a reliable signal in itself — but in the direction opposite to the original signal."**
- **What the computer is genuinely for**: automating rote work (data, charts, accounting, tax); displaying the same data
  many ways (point-and-figure, candlestick, close-only); effortless quantification of volume studies and the 50-/200-day
  moving averages that many participants watch; and above all **portfolio analysis** and automatic
  **marking-to-market**. On the last point he invokes Magee's *General Semantics of Wall Street* (now *Winning the
  Mental Game on Wall Street*): people keep "two sets of books in the head" — all profits, open and closed, but only
  *closed* losses. An electronic portfolio that refuses to participate in that self-deception "has much to recommend it."
- On the indicator zoo (stochastics, Bollinger Bands, %R, MACD, moving averages, price/volume divergence, RSI, VP Trend,
  TCI, OBV, trading bands, AccumDis): "there is a certain whiff of alchemy to some of them, and some have some
  usefulness sometimes. And, all systems work beautifully at least twice in their lives: in research, and in huge
  monumental Bull Markets." Also: "you can drive a nail with a screwdriver. And the inventor of a tool may be fabulously
  successful with it while its adopters lose their assets."
- "An excess of technical information … may be like the excess of fundamental information — another shell to hide the
  pea under." Number-driven models "cannot put the market in perspective. Only the human mind can do that."
- **Summary 1**: "The computer is a tool, a powerful tool, but a tool nonetheless… We use a mechanical ditch digger to
  dig a ditch, but not to figure out where the ditch should be."
- On the Internet: a cornucopia of brokers, advisors, databases, chat rooms, ECNs, electronic exchanges — and the
  question is priority, not availability. The level-headed investor "needs data, charts, and a connection to a trading
  place." Chaff: chat rooms, touts, punditry, and brokerage-house recommendations taken at face value (noting the
  conflict of interest that "blew up in their faces in the early 2000s resulting in many fines and some jail terms").
  Real-time data is "a way for the unsophisticated trader to go broke in real time."
- Advantage of electronic order entry: the data are handled once, which removes a layer of error — "and rest assured,
  errors occur and can be disastrous to trading."

## Chapter 17.2 — Advancements in Investment Technology (pp. 333–354) · *Bassetti*

Two parts: a survey of modern finance theory and practice, and a detailed manual on **CBOT DJIA futures and options on
futures**. The repeated refrain is that these instruments are essential knowledge *and* dangerous without training.

**Part 1 — Developments in finance theory and practice**

- **Options.** Since Black–Scholes (1973) the derivatives market has gone from negligible to trillions. "The investor
  who is not informed about options is playing with half a deck." The single most important fact: ~**30% of options
  expire worthless** (the 60–30–10 rule: 60% closed before expiration, 10% exercised, 30% long at expiration).
  Estimates are that **~90% of retail option traders lose money**, and the public is overwhelmingly a *buyer* while the
  professionals are sellers — "some syllogistic reasoning" is invited. 1987 anecdote: out-of-the-money puts bought at
  $0.625 on 16 October were worth hundreds on 19 October — and an Options Research client short naked puts lost **$57MM
  in 3 days** and nearly brought down a major Chicago bank. Legitimate uses: leverage (speculation), insurance
  (protective puts), and yield enhancement. On **covered calls**: writing a call on a stock in a clear *uptrend* "is to
  make oneself beloved of the broker" — the stock gets called away at the strike, "a sort of booby prize — a way of
  cutting your profits while increasing your costs." Only clearly range-bound markets justify it.
- **Quantitative analysis and pricing models.** Black–Scholes takes five parameters — strike, stock price, risk-free
  rate, time to expiration, volatility. Later models: Cox–Ross–Rubinstein, Black Futures. "Not knowing what these values
  are for the private investor is like not knowing where the present price is for a stock."
- **Futures on indexes.** Intense leverage, and unlike long options "a mishap in the market can result in more than the
  loss of margin" — a deficit account and losses over 100%.
- **Options on futures and indexes** — conservative as well as speculative use (e.g. buying an index put against a long
  SPDR position after a spike). "He must, in short, figure out how you lose."
- **Modern Portfolio Theory.** Its four processes: portfolio valuation, asset allocation, optimization, performance
  measurement. Bassetti's verdict for the general investor: "Not very [important]." He cites Mandelbrot ("A Multifractal
  Walk Down Wall Street") that MPT "discards about 5% of statistical experience as if it didn't exist," and that the
  discarded experience includes the **"10-sigma" market storms** blamed for portfolio failures "as though it is the
  fault of the data instead of the fault of the process."
- "Any number of little old ladies with a chart, a pencil, and previous editions of this book have beaten the pants off
  professional stock pickers with supercomputers and MPT and Nobel Laureates."

**Part 2 — Dow futures and futures options mechanics** (worked in detail): contract value = index × $10; cash
settlement via the Special Opening Quotation; daily **marking to market**; **fungibility** and the Clearing Corporation
removing counterparty risk; initial margin 3–5%; **cost of carry** and theoretical/fair value; arbitrage keeping futures
and cash aligned.

**Three worked situations** — (1) **portfolio protection**: seeing a broadening top in the Dow circa 2000, sell three
futures contracts against a $400,000 Dow portfolio to reverse an 80/20 equity/cash posture without liquidating, deferring
tax and avoiding slippage; (2) **increasing exposure**: buy one contract to "equitize" $100,000 of money-market holdings;
(3) **asset allocation**: spread short T-bond futures against long index futures to shift 50/50 to 75/25 without
touching the underlying portfolios.

**Options on Dow futures** — calls and puts, long and short; buyers have fixed risk (the premium), sellers "have
potentially unlimited risk. Catastrophic moves in the markets often bankrupt imprudent option sellers." Premium =
**intrinsic value + time value**; in/at/out-of-the-money; price is a function of futures price, strike, time, money
market rate and **volatility** — "of these variables, volatility is the only one that cannot be observed directly," hence
**implied volatility** (which Ch 27 suggests as a stop-distance input). Worked strategies: long calls in rising markets;
long puts to exploit reversals; **protective puts** placing a floor on an appreciated portfolio ("the similarity to life
insurance is striking. If you don't die, the premium is wasted. But if you do…"); **covered call writing** for yield in
clearly range-bound markets only; and **straddles** — a **long straddle** in coiling, low-volatility markets (the editor's
firm used one immediately before the 1987 crash "and collected disproportionate profits on a very low risk position"),
a **short straddle** when options are overvalued relative to expected volatility, with the warning about catastrophic
risk and the $57MM client.

# Part 2 — Trading Tactics

## Midword (pp. 355–358) — *John Magee*

A warning about expectations, built on James Bryant Conant's point that science does not arrive "pat, perfect, and ready
for use" — discovery takes form little by little. "Theories of market action are not conceived in a flash of inspiration;
they are built, step by step, out of the experience of traders and students."

- The neophyte's question "What is the method?" usually means "What can I buy to make a lot of money easily and quickly?"
  He starts with no theory, no experience and no basis for confidence; the first losing streak sends him away saying
  "It's no damn good."
- The shade-tobacco analogy: three ruined crops in a row wouldn't make a trained farmer conclude the business is
  worthless — because he would *know* he was right.
- **"No method that has ever been devised will unfailingly protect you against a loss, or sometimes even a painful
  succession of losses."** What you are looking for is the **probability inherent in the situation** — justified in
  continuing to draw from a bag of 700 white and 300 black beans even after drawing ten black in a row.
- "The problem stated and analyzed through this whole volume is not so much a matter of 'systems' as it is a matter of
  **philosophy**."
- The book "should not be given a quick 'once-over'… It should be read over and over, a number of times, and it should
  be consulted as a reference work."

## Chapter 18 — The Tactical Problem (pp. 359–366)

"It is possible to lose money in a Bull Market — and, likewise, to lose money trading short in a Bear Market." Strategy
can be 100% right and still fail without tactics. Why inexperienced traders do poorly: at the moment of buying they
have **no objective and no exit policy** in either direction, so profits evaporate and losses run. Add the psychological
handicap of immediate costs and the near-certainty of an adverse excursion — "even on a perfectly sound, wise trade, he
may see a 10% or more paper loss, before the expected favorable move gets under way."

**Three confusions to eliminate**

1. **A stock is not the company.** "The securities of a strong company are often weak; and sometimes the securities of
   a very weak concern are exceedingly strong."
2. **Don't attribute value from dividend yield alone.** Stocks have held a steady dividend while losing 75% of capital
   value; others made huge gains paying nothing. On this logic, non-payers would have no value at all. Technically,
   total gain = sale proceeds − cost + dividends; the split between income and capital is irrelevant.
3. **Don't mix "realized" and "accrued."** The investor who says he has no loss in a $30 stock now at $22 "because he
   has not sold it" will nonetheless speak of his *profit* in a stock at $45. "It is not being honest with yourself to
   use one method to conceal your mistakes and the other method to accentuate your successes." Use **accrued** —
   measured against today's close. Bassetti: with mark-to-market software, "avoidance of this knowledge can only be
   regarded as self-defeating."

**Other points**

- The technician is not committed to buy-and-hold: "The successful technician will never, for emotional causes, remain
  in a situation which, on the evidence at hand, is no longer tenable."
- Net gains from intermediate trends can exceed the whole net gain on the major trend even after extra commissions and
  higher short-term tax — but they "are not easily won."
- On mechanical systems: "it is not possible to 'beat the market' by the application of any simple mathematical system."
  Bassetti's qualification: objective systems *have* succeeded, but "the markets usually become aware of the success of
  these systems and develop counterstrategies to defeat them," so their performance tends to degenerate or fail.
- **The strongest argument for short-term tactics**: protection against panic markets. The long-term investor can see
  years of gains erased in days; the short-term trader is taken out by stops with moderate losses and keeps his capital
  for the new trend. Bassetti: "even long-term investors should not grin and bear a Bear Market."
- Scope: these methods fit odd lots up to a few thousand shares depending on liquidity, not 10,000-share blocks where
  your own orders move the price. "A mouse can go where an elephant can't."
- The Enron chart (Fig 201.0) carries the chapter's hardest lesson: "**get out of the stock when it reverses.** The
  corollary of that lesson is, **never buy a stock in a downtrend.** And there is even a more important lesson: **Never
  buy a stock when it is in a swan dive.**" University endowment managers and the Motley Fools held it all the way down.

## Chapter 18.1 — Strategy and Tactics for the Long-Term Investor (pp. 367–374) · *Bassetti*

Re-defines the vocabulary for a modern reader, then states a long-term strategy that is explicitly *not* buy-and-hold.

- **Then vs now**: Magee's "speculator" (who traded medium-term trends and bought unfashionable stocks) would today be
  called a *trader* or a *medium-term investor*; today's day trader is the nearest equivalent of the old-time
  speculator; the dividend-collecting "New Haven investor" is nearly extinct. "Only the passive … never trade at all and
  sit on their holdings during Bear Markets."
- **The strategy**: catch trends lasting months and years, "but this strategy does not intend to be sucked into long
  Bear Markets. Rather, portfolios are liquidated or hedged when Bear Market signals are received." Passive indexing
  (SPDRs, DIAMONDS, index funds, futures) is the most likely way to beat both the market and the funds — only ~20% of
  which outperform over the long term.
- Exit triggers, calibrated to temperament: Dow Theory signals; breaks of the 200-day moving average; or breaks of
  long-term/intermediate trendlines with a filter (Magee's 2%, possibly 3% or a volatility-derived figure). Emphatically:
  "'200' is a parameter and an example. Personal research may fit a better parameter to the actual market." And look at
  more than the single line — the chart patterns of the holdings and of the major indexes too.
- The trader's tactics are **not in conflict** with the long-term investor's: "As buying or selling time approaches the
  stops of the long-term investor, that investor becomes a trader." The same charting techniques work on tick data,
  which demonstrates "the **fractal** nature of both price data and the applicability of Magee-type technical analysis
  to it."
- **Scaling the rules to the horizon**: where the speculator uses the 3-days-away rule for basing points (Ch 28), a
  long-term investor might use a 4-week equivalent — then switch to the 3-day rule, or a daily stop just under the
  market, when a long bull market approaches a climax. "Beware though. Professionals look for stops just under the close
  of the previous day in situations such as these."
- **Rhythmic Investing** — roll the portfolio gradually from long to short in natural rhythm with the market, guided by
  the **Magee Evaluative Index** (Ch 38). "That is the preferred strategy of the authors and editor of this book."
- **The demolition of "one-decision investing"**: in 1972 the Street's best analysts picked a generation portfolio —
  Avon, Eastman Kodak, IBM, Polaroid, Sears Roebuck, Xerox. The chapter tabulates the twenty-year outcome. IBM was
  recommended at a decade high, fell more than 50%, doubled from the recommendation, and ended twenty years ~40% *below*
  it. Xerox "was recommended at a place where it should have been sold instead of bought." "There are bulls, bears, and
  ostriches."
- Summary of the long-term investor's discipline: market leaders and index shares as ballast, a little speculative
  spice; hedge against secondaries if temperament allows; and **no tolerance for weak, losing or underperforming
  stocks** — "they are the shortest of his trades." The two least-understood maxims: "cut losses and let profits run,"
  and "**buy strength, sell weakness**."
- "This is the greatest difference between professional and general investors — professionals have no bias against the
  short side."

## Chapter 19 — The All-Important Details (pp. 375–378)

Deliberately mundane and, Bassetti admits, the kind of "cracker-barrel wisdom" he initially dismissed and came to
respect. "The details of how and when you keep the charts will not guarantee you profits. But if you fail to work out
these details in such a way as to make your work easy, as part of a regular systematic routine, you cannot expect to
keep up your charts properly, or to make any profits either."

- **Set a fixed time every day and let nothing interfere, ever, or you are lost.**
- Practical ergonomics: shadowless overhead light (two 40-watt fluorescents), a large clear surface, a slightly lower
  working height (28–29″), a dozen well-sharpened pencils set aside the instant one dulls. "The wrong kind of pencil can
  tire you and irritate you more than you realize."
- Seven-ring binders (two- and three-ring tear pages); file completed sheets chronologically; keep folders for brokers'
  slips, dividend notices, clippings, and **notes on your own methods and analyses**.
- Keep a complete record of every purchase, sale and dividend — for taxes, and for "all the statistical information you
  need to judge the results of your trading operations."
- Bassetti's restatement: **"Be serious. Be methodical. Be disciplined. Be business-like."** Anyone who succeeds without
  these "is the recipient of blind luck."
- His computer corollary: keep it simple. "Get the data. Draw a chart. Analyze the patterns. Consider the volume. Draw
  the appropriate analytical lines." Decorating charts with number-driven studies "can lead the general investor astray —
  and into confusion and indecision." Often a printed chart with hand-drawn lines reads better than the screen.

## Chapter 20 — The Kind of Stocks We Want: The Speculator's Viewpoint (pp. 379–382)

Simple specification: a stock whose price will move over a wide enough range to make trading worthwhile. Stability is a
virtue for an investor and a defect for a trader — stable stocks "move in rounding, sluggish undulations" rather than
making "the sharp, clear chart patterns of the more speculative issues," and commissions eat narrow swings.

- The **leverage** mechanism explained by example: a company whose preferred takes a fixed 6% leaves the common with
  everything else — 50¢ one year, $2.00 the next. The preferred sits still; the common can quadruple.
- Sources of speculative swing: an inherently uncertain business, *or* senior obligations shearing off the "sure" profit,
  *or* a small/closely-held float. "The nature of the business itself does not always account for the habits of the
  stock."
- **"Most stocks have a fairly well-defined 'swing' power, and you can usually determine by past performance how a stock
  will behave in the future as to the extent of its swing."** This is the thread that leads to the Sensitivity Index.
- A short sale implies nothing about the country or the company — only that the stock may be temporarily overpriced.
- Bassetti's modernisation: drop the loaded word "speculative"; describe stocks as **wide-ranging or narrow-ranging,
  volatile or non-volatile**, and compare them by **beta and historical volatility**.

## Chapter 20.1 — The Kind of Stocks We Want: The Long-Term Investor's Viewpoint (pp. 383–390) · *Bassetti*

The chapter that makes the rest of the 9th edition's strategy possible: you can now buy the averages.

- **SPDRs (SPY)** — introduced on the AMEX in 1993, an exchange-traded unit investment trust on the S&P 500; by 2000
  ~$15bn and >100,000,000 shares outstanding. **DIAMONDS (DIA)** — 1998, the same thing on the DJIA. Plus WEBS and
  ~30 other AMEX "Index Shares," and futures/options on the Dow (CBOT), S&P/Nikkei/Mini-S&P/Midcap/Russell 2000/NASDAQ
  100 (CME), and S&P 100/500 options (CBOE).
- Construction: SPY represents 1/10 of a basket of the S&P stocks, trades like a stock, pays quarterly dividends, allows
  reinvestment, and the trust runs into the 22nd century.
- **Why this matters**: instant maximum diversification for small capital (the S&P 500 is ~69% of NYSE value — "literally
  buying the American economy"); dampened risk; **tax control** the investor keeps himself (unlike a fund, where managers'
  rebalancing creates liabilities); and, because the trust is effectively perpetual, no need to realise gains at all —
  bear markets handled by *hedging* instead.
- **Technical regularity** is the fourth advantage: Magee observed the Dow averages were "very regular and dependable
  from the technical point of view," so "the investor in the Index Shares may have a smoother time technically than a
  trader of an individual stock."
- Bassetti's verdict on index funds: "hiring a management company to run an indexing strategy is imprudent and a waste
  of capital. Much better for the investor to invest directly AND to exit the market when uptrends end and reverse. This
  is a much better strategy than 'passive indexing' which cleverly manages to capture both losses and profits in the
  Averages."
- Keep Magee's admonition in view: **"it is a market of stocks, not a stock market."** And note the trade-off — these
  instruments will be less profitable than an astutely chosen individual stock (Qualcomm +240% vs the S&P's +24% in
  1999–2000) and far less damaging than a badly chosen one (−75% from Qualcomm's top). "Traders in Qualcomm tended to
  obsess… while investors in the SPDRs reviewed it once a week or less… Then they slept at night and had eupeptic
  digestion."
- Practical shortlist for the general investor: **DIA, SPY, QQQQ**. Mid-caps, Nikkei and the rest come into play only
  when chasing sector rotation and short-term cycles.

## Chapter 21 — Selection of Stocks to Chart (pp. 391–394)

The fundamentalist must concentrate on a few stocks; the technician should do the opposite and keep a **large and
diversified portfolio of charts**, because "the characteristics of the stocks themselves, as they act in the market, are
more important to him than what these companies make or what they are earning."

- The two-houses analogy: a fine $200,000 home and a shabby $200,000 six-family tenement. There is no question which is
  the better *house*; the market for apartments may make the tenement the better *investment*.
- A conservative business can still have a wildly swinging common — senior obligations, a small issue, or closely-held
  stock create leverage.
- **How many charts?** "The more stocks you chart, the more good opportunities you will have," because most charts say
  nothing most of the time. 20–30 for 15–30 minutes a day; 100 is much better; up to 300 if charting is most of your
  work. **"Don't start anything you can't finish"** — better to start too few and add.
- Chart listed, actively traded stocks: unlisted issues give only bid/offer with no recorded sales or volume. Exchange
  listing also guarantees disclosure and rules. NYSE examples dominate the book, but **"So far as the chart action is
  concerned, the patterns and their meanings will be the same"** on any exchange, in any country *(the editor's italics)*.
- Bassetti's modern answer to "how many": screen on an exception basis — *"Computer, show me all the stocks which are
  above their 50-day moving average and which have unusual volume."* Candidates: the most-active lists, optionable
  stocks with a radical change in implied volatility, volume spikes against average volume, and filters on the 14-, 44-,
  150- and 200-day moving averages (14 because some professionals think the public thinks in that frame, 44 because some
  think funds do, the others "because everyone thinks they are important").

## Chapter 22 — Selection of Stocks to Chart, Continued (pp. 395–400)

How to actually assemble the list. **Maximum diversity of industry group**, because stocks in a group tend to make the
same patterns at the same time (when Allis-Chalmers makes a triangle, Deere, Minneapolis-Moline, Harvester and Case tend
to follow). With a limited number of charts, spread across groups so that something is always moving decisively.

**Price level.** Lower-priced issues make much larger percentage moves: the same industry move that carries a $5 stock
to 10 (+100%) may carry a $100 stock to 140 (+40%) — 2½× the gain on the same capital. But low-priced stocks also fall
much faster, may be erratic and thin, carry higher percentage commissions, and may be ineligible for margin.

- **Best trading range: the 20s**, workable down through the teens and up through the 40s. "Above 40 and below 10, you
  will have to have strong reasons for trading, which might be, of course, ample capital." Don't pick the group's $150
  outlier when peers trade at 15, 28 and 37 — nor its $2 and $4 members.
- **Individual habit is separate from price level.** Two stocks at 30 may react 20% and 50% respectively to the same
  market setback, and "the same stocks which make these relatively different reactions in one setback will make about
  the same moves, relative to each other, in other setbacks." The ones that fall hardest also skyrocket hardest, and
  **these habits "seem to be quite stable over periods of many years."** Quantified as the **Sensitivity Index**
  (Appendix A) — the historical predecessor of **beta**.
- **Eliminate thin stocks**: spotty charts full of gaps and no-sale days, moves of several points on a few hundred
  shares, 1–2 point changes between single hundreds, and a punishing bid–ask spread. "They are difficult to trade in
  because they are hard to buy and hard to sell."
- Group tendencies: foods and tobaccos are less responsive; rails, liquors and airlines very responsive. But don't
  agonise — "even if you took the first 50 or 100 stocks in the listed issues, you would have among them at least 25
  good trading stocks."
- Bassetti's simplification: trade the **group index** rather than picking a winner within it — "almost never as
  profitable as a well-chosen individual issue, but … always better than a badly chosen individual issue." Or follow
  only SPY and DIA: "If it were not only bought, but also sold, or hedged, the market would be outperformed. This would
  be a simple investor's life indeed."

## Chapter 23 — Choosing and Managing High-Risk Stocks: Tulip Stocks, Internet Sector, and Speculative Frenzies (pp. 401–418) · *Bassetti*

The 9th edition's set-piece on manias, anchored on MacKay's *Extraordinary Popular Delusions* — "A golden bait hung
temptingly out before the people, and one after the other, they rushed to tulip-marts, like flies around a honey pot."

**The governing principle**, stated in italics in the text: ***all of the techniques and methods described in this book
remain valid for dealing with these kinds of stocks.*** What changes is the *tempo* — "accumulation, attraction, mark up,
mania, blow-off will occur in compressed time spans."

- **The sector becomes a market unto itself** with its own technical factors.
- **By the IPO the insiders are already distributing.** When Palm was spun off from 3Com in 2000 only **3%** of the
  shares were sold, creating artificial scarcity and giving Palm a market value briefly exceeding that of 3Com, which
  owned most of it. Add "laddering" — underwriters requiring inside clients to buy more in the open market at higher
  prices: "a clever way to throw gasoline on a raging bonfire."
- "It is the height of foolishness to enter a market order to buy on the issue of the IPO. An issue going public at 12
  might trade on the opening at 50 in these frenzies — a sign to the savvy technician that the sheep are headed for the
  shearing shed."
- **Management techniques**: progressive stops via basing points and the 3-days-away rule (Ch 28); additionally **raise
  stops on each new percentage high**; some light pyramiding and scaling out on continuous new highs; and in the
  blow-off phase, exit on a **long reversal day or a key reversal pattern** (Ch 10.1) "and then go to the beach."
- **Three caveats** — (1) commit no more than **5–10%** of capital; (2) **don't be early on the short side**, since
  there may be a second stage to the rocket; (3) emotional involvement "can lead to a broken heart."
- Profits are available **both ways**: "The crowd will only think of the riches to be made long. Professionals and
  skilled technicians … will take the profits on the short side."
- Treat these as "interesting aberrations in the early part of their lives. Take the money and run." After the blow-off,
  crash and a reasonable bottom, *then* look for investment possibilities — "there is too much potential in all of them
  for some phoenix not to rise from the ashes."
- Charts worked through: Microsoft (bull trap, Department-of-Justice gaps, runaway days — and the editor's own admitted
  lesson, "never leave a chart unanalyzed, even if the implications are obvious"), Palm/PLMO/PSRC, 3Com, Oracle,
  Inktomi, Emulex ("from 110 to 10"), Amazon (weekly and daily — "in cases of speculative blow-off, trendlines are of
  little use"; sell *strength*), Cisco (a student who worked there exited on the trendlines and saved thousands), and
  Google (rumoured at $200, IPO'd at $85, "and Google went to 475!!").
- The closing lesson, which he says must be learned and relearned *ad infinitum*: **"Trust the chart. Ignore the story."**

## Chapter 24 — The Probable Moves of Your Stocks (pp. 419–424)

Quantifies the habit observed in Ch 22. "We do not know whether a year from now Glenn Martin will be moving up or down,
but we do know, and it is one of the most dependable things we know, that whichever way it is going, it will be covering
ground much faster than American Telephone and Telegraph."

- **The operative threshold**: "In order to be in a position to make a profit, you should see the probability of at
  least a **15%** move in your stock."
- Method: compare a stock's percentage moves over several years with the market's. You cannot say "this stock will now
  move up 25%," but you can say with confidence "if the market as a whole advances 10%, this stock will probably advance
  about 25%."
- The worked example: Corn Products (a food stock) and Schenley (a distiller) at the same average price of ~64½ over the
  same 18 months. CFG ranged 58½–71 (12½ points); SH ranged 28½–100 (71½ points). $1,000 became $1,210 in CFG and
  **$3,510** in SH — a gain more than ten times larger, unmargined. **Sensitivity Index: CFG 0.58, SH 2.05.**
- Bassetti maps this onto modern statistics: the **Sensitivity Index ≈ beta** (systematic risk, available from Value
  Line / finance.yahoo.com, formula given), and **Normal Range-for-Price ≈ volatility** (individual risk; the standard
  deviation of returns, formula given step by step). He notes volatility is "an extremely sophisticated subject" whose
  serious use is in options arbitrage and floor trading, and that the private investor mainly needs to know the dangers:
  "Before venturing into 'volatility plays,' the newcomer should take a postgraduate course."

## Chapter 25 — Two Touchy Questions (pp. 425–432)

**Question 1: margin.** The anti-margin argument is half-thought-through. Buying a *sensitive, highly leveraged* stock
outright accomplishes almost exactly what buying a conservative stock on margin does. "Very often, despite his feeling
that outright purchase is more conservative than margin buying, he is a speculator at heart."

- Worked comparison: UVW (Sensitivity 1.50) bought outright at 20 and rising to 30 = +50%; XYZ bought at 20 on 70%
  margin and rising to 28 = **+57%**. Margin merely accentuates sensitivity.
- "The effect of margin use is simply to accentuate or increase the sensitivity of a situation… The danger in margin
  lies in cases where the customer grossly overextends himself."
- A mortgage, or any business financed partly with borrowed money, is structurally the same transaction.
- **Margin calls "will seldom, if ever, come up if you protect yourself properly by maintaining stops at all times."**
  And if a trade has gone far enough bad to reach minimum maintenance, "take your loss and forget it; not try to meet
  the margin call."
- 10% margin is not a path from $1,000 to $1,000,000 — "in many cases, your perfectly logical purchase would sag enough
  to wipe you out entirely before going ahead to the normal advance you expected." (Bassetti: "In a nutshell, the risk
  of trading commodities and futures.")

**Question 2: short selling.** "Six out of seven you meet, who have bought or sold stocks, will tell you that they would
never sell a stock short under any conditions, at any time."

- The asymmetry that creates the opportunity: stocks advance about **two-thirds** of the time and decline about a third,
  yet over long periods levels rise and fall about the same amount. Therefore **they must come down much faster than
  they go up** — declines are steeper — and "profits can be made faster on the downside of the market than on the
  upside."
- Why the public is always bullish: most news, rumours and press comment concern "the brighter side of industry."
- The ethical objections ("selling America short," profiting from disaster, manipulator's trick) are "so much nonsense."
  "Money is a commodity, just as much as a share of stock. There is no moral or practical difference between borrowing
  money to buy stock because you believe that the latter will go up in value in terms of the former, and borrowing stock
  in order to 'buy' money because you believe the latter is going to go up in value in terms of the former."
- A magazine publisher taking cash for a subscription is making something like a short sale.
- **A large short interest strengthens a market**: "Every short seller is a potential buyer," which cushions bad breaks
  and can produce a fast short-covering rally. Some operators buy *because* the short interest is large.
- The "unlimited loss" objection "sounds much more alarming than it really is. Stocks do not go up without limit all of
  a sudden. It is just as easy to set a stop on the loss you are willing to take on a short-side transaction as it is on
  a long purchase."
- Mechanics: a short sale is necessarily a margin transaction; the short pays the dividends; execution requires a rising
  market (**the uptick rule** — Bassetti: ETFs, some HOLDRS and futures are exempt); and a slight extra tax applies
  (short gains don't qualify for long-term treatment). Professionals get interest on short proceeds; private investors
  generally don't — one of the edges.
- The verdict: "from every practical angle, a short sale is exactly the same thing (although in a reverse direction) as
  a long purchase, with no greater risk, with actually somewhat greater chance of quick profit, and differing only in
  details of execution."
- Bassetti: hedge-fund proliferation is evidence of professional frustration with long-only mandates; short selling
  remains "a limited technique — to the disadvantage of the nonprofessional."

## Chapter 26 — Round Lots or Odd Lots? (pp. 433–434)

Magee's original question (the cost penalty of odd lots) is declared obsolete: full diversification is now available in
odd lots of SPDRs and DIAMONDS, and commission-free brokers exist (monetising order flow instead). **The real modern
question is position size — risk and portfolio management.** The chapter's whole value is one formula, credited to
William Scott:

> **The Scott Procedure.** Decide what percentage of capital to risk on a trade (professionals commonly 2–3%). With
> $100,000 and a 3% factor, that is $3,000 at risk. If you buy a $20 stock and defend it with a stop $5 away:
> **$3,000 ÷ $5 = 600 shares.** With a $10 stop: $3,000 ÷ $10 = 300 shares.

"Thus we adjust our trade size to fit our risk parameters. This is a simple, practical, elegant way to implement risk
control at the individual trade level."

## Chapter 27 — Stop Orders (pp. 435–440)

Two different uses of the same mechanism.

**1 — The Protective Stop.** "At best, it is not a happy subject. Stop orders of this type are like fire extinguishers."
Wherever you place it, it will seem to be touched off at the worst possible moment — the stock will break through, catch
your stop, and then build its base or make new highs. "No matter. You had your reasons for setting the stop. The stock
did not act the way it should have." Without one, "what started to be a small reaction, and an annoying but trivial
loss, has turned out to be a ruinous catastrophe."

**The stop-distance formula.** Stop distance must scale with both price level and the stock's own habits — you would not
use the same percentage under an 80 blue chip and an 8 speculative issue. Anchored on an arbitrary reference of a
median-sensitivity stock at 25, whose Normal Range-for-Price index is 15.5, protected 5% below the last minor bottom:

> **Stop % = (Normal Range-for-Price ÷ 15.5) × Sensitivity Index × 5%**

Worked: a stock at 5 (Normal Range 24) → 5% × 24/15.5 ≈ **7.5%**. Apply a Sensitivity Index of 1.33 → **10%**; of 2.00 →
**15%**; of 0.66 → **5%**. A **Table of Stop Distances** by price class and sensitivity (conservative / median /
speculative) is supplied for those who won't do the arithmetic. **Never less than a 5% interval**, even for the most
conservative high-priced stock. Bassetti's proposed modernisation: base the distance on **implied volatility** (solving
Black–Scholes for volatility given market price), giving a dynamic adjustment.

**Rules of operation**

- Mark the stop level on the chart as a horizontal line **the moment the commitment is made**, and maintain it until the
  trade is closed. "We will always, always set our stop level at the moment we make the commitment."
- Longs: at the computed distance **below the last minor bottom**. Shorts: above the last minor top.
- **Protective stops under longs are never moved down** (and never up over shorts) — except to adjust for ex-dividend or
  ex-rights, by the amount of the distribution.
- Move the stop up as soon as a new basing point is established (Ch 28).

**2 — The Progressive Stop.** For taking profits out of a move that has reached its objective, exceeded it, or shown a
danger signal. The situation: after a good rise the stock develops **exceptional volume**, often right at a trendline,
pattern border or resistance area. Nine times out of ten that is the end of the move; occasionally it is the start of a
near-vertical breakaway. Selling at market risks missing the latter.

- **The mechanism**: cancel the protective stop and enter a **day-only stop ⅛ point under the closing price**. If it is
  caught, you are out near the top; if the stock gaps up and runs, you are still aboard and you repeat the ⅛-point
  day-stop under each successive close until one is hit. Worked example: risking ⅛ point on the first day and netting an
  extra 2⅝ points, all of it net gain since commissions are the same either way.
- **The one exception**: do *not* apply a tight stop on the day the stock makes a new high running entirely through the
  previous minor top and closing above it — that generally means the move isn't finished. But if heavy volume reappears,
  even the very next day, protect it.
- An alternative noted in a footnote: leave the stop at the same point each day until another blow-off signal (heavy
  volume, a gap, or a one-day reversal) appears, then move it to ⅛ under that day's close. "This method often works out
  somewhat better than the continuously progressive near stop."
- Also indicated when extreme volume appears mid-channel *before* a new high — "definitely a warning and a threat,"
  especially with a gap or one-day reversal.
- "Heavy volume" always means heavy relative to that stock's own recent volume.
- Who needs stops: the trader without time or long experience is "protected from his own unwillingness to close out the
  bad holding." The experienced technician who will reliably act on a confirmed turn has less need of them, and some
  advantage in not using them (stops are occasionally caught by false moves). Bassetti's test: "The reader may determine
  if he meets this criterion by examining his portfolio to see if he has ever let a loss run, or allowed a significant
  profit to slip away. If so he… is not proven."
- "But it is a thousand times better for the person who is not sure of his methods to be stopped out early, then to be
  left holding a stock bought at, say 60, when it has declined to 29 — or to 5!"

## Chapter 28 — What Is a Bottom — What Is a Top? (pp. 441–445)

Not major or intermediate turns — the **minor** tops and bottoms from which stop levels, trendlines, objectives and
support/resistance are all measured. "They are of prime importance to us as traders." Bassetti's 9th-edition note: think
in terms of **higher highs and higher lows** (a bull trend) and watch for that pattern breaking.

> **The "Three Days Away" Rule.** A bottom is established when the stock has moved three days away from the day marking
> the suspected low: **the entire price range for three full days must lie entirely above the high of the low day.**
> (Reversed for tops in declining markets.) If the low day ranged 24–25, you need three days with no price below 25⅛.

- This gives both the rule for setting the original stop and the rule for *advancing* it: as soon as the stock is three
  days away from a new bottom, move the stop below that bottom at the Ch 27 distance.

**Basing Points** — the levels from which stops are measured.

- In a bull move, use **both** the bottom of each minor reaction **and** each minor top as basing points. Mirror in a
  bear market.
- A move of 15%+ in the primary direction followed by a retracement of **at least 40%** of the distance from the
  previous basing point certainly gives a basing point once the primary move resumes. But a retracement of *less* than
  40% — even a week or more of marking time at the same level — also qualifies **provided the volume indications are
  right**.
- **The three expected volume peaks**: (1) the day of breakout from a pattern or period of inaction, especially upside;
  (2) the day prices go into new ground past the last minor top (bull) or minor bottom (bear); (3) the day the minor move
  completes. *"Extra heavy volume on any other day during a move in the Primary Direction is likely to indicate that the
  move is at an end."*
- **What to watch for in a correction is the decline of volume.** If trading shrinks steadily (if irregularly) for some
  days while the stock reacts or merely makes no progress, you are justified in calling it a minor correction. The
  correction typically carries to the previous minor high, to the basic trendline, or to the parallel — and a *horizontal*
  correction tends to run out until it meets one of those lines.
- For an ambiguous breakout from a period of vacillation, **treat the appearance of high volume as the breakout signal
  and set the basing point at the low immediately preceding it.**
- The hard case — a long runaway move that starts a flag, is bought on a 40% correction with declining volume, and then
  simply keeps sinking without rallies or volume clues. Fall back on support levels formed on the way down in the
  previous trend rather than holding a stop under the extreme bottom. And conclude: **don't try to buy corrections after
  long runaway moves** unless (a) the stock is well above good support usable as a basing point, or (b) it is above all
  prices for several years and moving "in the clear." Either way, demand "pronounced and conspicuous drying up of volume."
- **The warning about staircases**: a regular trend with good trendlines, 40–50% reactions and shrinking corrective
  volume invites the assumption it will continue. "But trends do not go on forever. **Any Minor Top may be the last.**"
  Volume again helps: be exceedingly suspicious of volume much higher than on any preceding minor top. "The final, or
  the next-to-final, 'blow-off' of a trend will usually show more volume than any of the Minor blow-offs along the way;
  and when you see such climactic volume, you should prepare to retire into your shell." Return weeks or months later,
  after a 40%+ correction of the whole intermediate move, with the stock quiet.
- Bassetti: "In a book composed of nothing but important chapters this Chapter 28 might not get the emphasis it deserves…
  the procedure outlined here is of absolutely basic importance in analyzing and trading trends."

## Chapter 28.1 — Basing Point Case Analyzed, Illustrated (pp. 446–450) · *Bassetti*

Codifies Magee's procedure into four numbered rules and then walks a full bull market (Apple, 1987) from entry to exit
with a 27-point key.

> **The four rules**
> 1. A high is made, recognised by no higher prices occurring for the moment.
> 2. Prices recede and a low is made — found by watching each day after the previous high until no lower prices are made.
>    As prices rise again, note each day whose range lies **completely outside** the range of the low-day candidate.
> 3. When **three such days** are observed before a new low is made, mark the candidate day a **Basing Point** and raise
>    the stop to **6% (or x%) under the low of the basing point day**.
> 4. If a new high is **3% greater** than the previous high, a new basing point is found at **the low of the new-high day**.

- **Why the filter matters**: "locals and hedge funds and those who profit from volatility know that the previous low is
  where investors and traders set their stops. So… if they see an opportunity to take out an important low they will do
  it." Bruce Kovner, asked by Jack Schwager where he set his stops: *"Where they're hard to get to."* "A stop set on a
  basing point with a prudently calculated filter is hard to get to, unless the market has truly reversed direction."
- The conceptual identity: **"what are Basing Points but the marking of highs and lows in full realization that a
  pattern of higher highs and higher lows is a bull trend, and when that pattern changes to one of lower highs and lower
  lows the trend is changing… This is the principle behind Dow Theory."** And a pattern of lower highs and lower lows
  means inevitably that the trendline has been broken.
- Also: "what is a long-term moving average but a lagging stop with a filter built in?"
- Candour about Magee's original Figure 210: it "preserves unexplainable conundrums and conflicts carefully preserved
  since the earliest editions. The reader is urged to take it as a concept rather than using it as a lesson."
- The simplified teaching version uses **only wave lows** for stops; the full procedure (advancing stops on new highs
  too) is left to advanced traders, with the trade-off noted: a mid-trend blow-off can force a premature exit.
- Narrative highlights from the worked chart: a volume day as the "wake-up call"; a run day on heavy volume breaking a
  horizontal line as the entry; probing by locals to chase out "arrivistes"; weak basing points from shallow
  retracements; candidates that fail the 3-day rule; the **inviolable rule that stops are never lowered** even when a new
  low undercuts an old one (the 5% filter keeps the position); a flag serving as a basing point; a too-steep trendline
  as a warning; and finally a support line pierced on extraordinary volume taking out the basing-point stop — "clearly
  time to exit the train."

## Chapter 29 — Trendlines in Action (pp. 451–460)

Magee's complete colour-coded trading system, built on the recognition that **most patterns are manifestations of trend
action**: a symmetrical triangle is two trends meeting; an ascending triangle is an uptrend meeting a resistance level;
an H&S is the end of an uptrend and the beginning of a downtrend; a rectangle is a horizontal parallel channel.

**Notation.** Mark the **upper** trendline (across tops) in **blue**; the **lower** (across bottoms) in **red**. A
**Blue Parallel** is a dotted blue line drawn parallel to the Blue Trend through the intervening bottom; a **Red
Parallel** likewise below. Since tops and bottoms alternate, you alternately draw a blue and a red line as each new
extreme is established. (Bassetti apologises for the black-and-white printing and promises a colour PDF.)

**Buying — preparatory signals** (a buying opportunity may be forming)

- Penetration of the Blue Trend to a new high closing (in most cases). *Note: the simple breaking of a descending Blue
  Trendline, with no other pattern or indication, is **not** sufficient evidence of reversal to justify a commitment.*
- Contact with an ascending Blue Trend when the Red Trend is also ascending, **provided the trends do not converge**
  (parallel or divergent channel — a converging pair suggests a wedge, which is bearish).
- Contact with a horizontal Blue Trend when the Red Trend is horizontal or ascending (rectangle, ascending triangle).
- Penetration of a descending Blue Trend **on volume** when the Red Trend is ascending (symmetrical triangle).

**Buying — execution**

- If the previous Blue Trend was *ascending*: draw the Blue Parallel and buy at or near it.
- If it was horizontal or descending (emerging from rectangles, triangles, reversal patterns): **buy on a reaction of
  40–45%** of the distance from the last minor bottom to the extreme top of the most recent move.

**Liquidating a long**

- Set the stop (Ch 27) immediately on execution; it may be raised by the three-days-away rule, never lowered (except
  ex-dividend).
- A close **below a previous minor bottom** (establishing a descending Red Trend) → sell on tight progressive stops.
- Unusually high volume on any day of the advance **before** the Blue Trend is topped or a new high closing made → sell
  on tight progressive stops.
- High volume **on** the day it tops the Blue Trend or makes a new high closing → **hold.** If heavy volume recurs the
  next day or any later day → sell on tight progressive stops.
- The volume signal (often with a one-day reversal or exhaustion gap) frequently appears **on or near the Red
  Parallel** — "watch especially for this volume indication as a sign of a good profit-taking point." Absent a volume
  signal, the Red Parallel *is* the objective, at a limit or on tight progressive stops.
- If there is no volume signal and the move reaches neither the Blue Trend nor a new high, you are probably in a
  triangle: wait for the breakout, maintaining stop protection.

**Short selling** mirrors all of the above (penetration of the Red Trend to a new low closing; contact with a descending
Red Trend when the Blue is also descending and non-converging; etc.), executing on a 40–45% rally, covering on tight
progressive stops.

**Additional suggestions**

- When a level that looks like a minor top or bottom **stalls in a very narrow range for 3 weeks or more** with no price
  or volume signal, treat that congestion as a key area and move protective stops to it as the basing point, against the
  possibility that the eventual move is the wrong way.
- After a series of trend moves each on heavier volume than the corrections, a move with **extraordinary** volume means
  the intermediate trend's final blow-off: cancel plans for further commitments in that direction. "One of the most
  common errors, and one of the easiest to fall into, is to mistake a Climactic Top or Bottom for a normal confirmation
  or preparatory signal for a new commitment."

**General policy for trading in the major trend**

- **A.** Always trade in the direction of the major/primary Dow trend as indicated at the time.
- **B.** If the two Dow averages disagree, trade in the direction of the last established primary trend **but only in the
  component still following it**.
- **C.** Check group averages; trade in the primary direction when the group's trend corresponds.
- **D.** Trade a stock when its own chart indicates a trend in the primary direction and the technical picture indicates
  a probable move that way.
- Make all new commitments **on the reaction or rally following the signalling move** — except on a primary reversal from
  bull to bear, when short sales may be made at market immediately.
- *Exception*: after an extended move or series of moves, when exhaustion and reversal signs appear in individual charts,
  counter-trend commitments may be made with objectives **limited to a correction of the preceding intermediate move**.

## Chapter 30 — Use of Support and Resistance (pp. 461–466)

Puts Ch 13 to work. It cannot be reduced to a formula — "here you will depend very largely on experience and
observation" — but a usable rule emerges.

> **Buy on the reaction to support after the first breakout. Buy on the reaction to the first minor peak after the next
> move. But do not buy on the reaction to the second minor peak.** After three such steps in the primary direction, "it
> is time to look for an Intermediate Correction, or at least an important period of Consolidation."

- The mechanism: each reaction tends to stop at the level of the preceding peak (or, in declines, each rally at the
  preceding bottom). But "we all know that this sort of thing does not continue indefinitely" — as the stock runs
  15→19→21→25→30→40, selling by long-term holders becomes increasingly tempting. Regard each successive step "with
  increasing suspicion."
- After passing up the third trade, **wait for the intermediate reaction** — which may take many weeks — and look to buy
  at or somewhat above the **intermediate support**, usually the top of the advance preceding the one just completed.
  Mirror in bear markets, with no more than three steps of decline expected before an intermediate recovery; put out
  shorts near the previous intermediate bottom.
- **When has support failed?** This "can be a very painful" question, because the slow drifting decline that never
  rallies is the dangerous one. The answer is procedural, not analytical: **"you should decide, at the time you make the
  original commitment, just how much leeway you are prepared to give the stock."** Then you are not tempted to postpone
  the decision day by day.
  - Against **minor** peaks/bottoms: measure from the *extreme* high/low of the supporting level (often the intraday
    extreme, not the close) and set the Ch 27 stop. Penetration that far is presumptive evidence the support won't work.
  - Against **major or intermediate** support: allow more leeway. Estimate the zone's **core or axis** visually — the
    volume-weighted "centre of gravity" where a great many shares changed hands — and set the stop beyond *that*.
- "And the most painful part of all is that sometimes, the moment you have sold and taken your loss, the stock will come
  to life and complete what would have been an extremely profitable move. You might just as well prepare yourself for
  this sort of disappointment; for it will happen to you."
- **Reconciling the three entry signals.** After a move to a new minor top, expect a reaction (1) of **40–50%** of that
  move, (2) to the **basic trendline**, and (3) to the **previous supporting minor top**. In a great many cases all three
  nearly coincide. The rule: **enter on whichever factor indicates the smallest reaction, and place the stop beyond the
  greatest reaction indicated by any of the three.**
- After an *intermediate* decline, lean more heavily on support/resistance than on a percentage or a trendline: check
  weekly/monthly charts for the major trend and the important zones, then the dailies. As the corrective move comes
  within 4–5% of the zone, an **extremely heavy-volume day — especially a one-day reversal — means commit at once**
  (with a stop); otherwise commit when the chart hesitates or flattens, or within 3% of the level.
- Taking profits after such an entry is harder, because three outcomes are open: the stock may form a line/rectangle at
  the level and then penetrate it (get out at once on a clear close through); it may make a small move and return for
  another attempt (prepare to get out on definite penetration); or the major trend may resume on increased volume — in
  which case set tight stops on the first heavy-volume day (except a breakout day), and once that signal has appeared
  you are again justified in buying the following minor corrections.
- Bassetti's dissent: this chapter is "really most pertinent to position building and pyramiding," or to an issue you
  noticed late. **"I feel strongly that the serious trader should not miss the original breakout. Chasing moving trains
  is never a healthy activity"** — and with computers monitoring thousands of stocks for gaps and suspicious volume,
  there is little excuse. (9th-ed. concession: "human frailty being what it is we will all find ourselves chasing a
  train at some time or other.")
- He also notes an attempt to *quantify* support/resistance: Adaptick's **Powerstrike** for Metastock, built on the
  observation that trading and support/resistance in optionable stocks cluster around important **option strike prices**.
  The battlefield metaphor: "Since the time of the Greeks battles have occurred time and again in the same physical
  locations… Option strike prices have the same attractiveness for traders that a good battleground has for a military
  commander."

## Chapter 31 — Not All in One Basket (pp. 467–468)

Diversification "is important because technical patterns do not always carry out their original promise." Without it a
false move affecting only your holdings can hurt you while the rest of the market advances.

- But intelligent diversification requires costing it out. Round-trip costs are a **higher percentage** in low-priced
  stocks, on smaller share counts, and on smaller total capital. A portfolio replicating the whole Dow on small capital
  would mean half a dozen shares of each — "entirely too costly for short-term trading."
- Suggested scaling: $1,000–2,000 of capital → units of about $500, confined to odd lots of stocks at 40 or higher.
  Larger capital → larger units and a lower price range. **"8 or 10 stocks should be a maximum for an active trading
  account."** Doubling or tripling unit size doesn't raise costs — it usually reduces them.
- **Bassetti's update**: diversification is now cheap via **SPDRs, DIAMONDS and similar instruments**. Mutual funds
  create costs index shares do not — management fees, expenses, slippage, spread, turnover, and tax on realised gains —
  "costs [that] may be avoided by the careful independent investor." An internationally prominent trader told him "that
  his respectable trading fortune amounts to what brokers and specialists would have made off him if he had been a
  member of the general public."
- "The message is extremely clear. The general investor must control his costs. The more frequently he trades the
  greater his chances of having his capital ground to hamburger meat by brokers, specialists, floor traders, market
  makers, tax authorities, Exchanges, etc."

## Chapter 32 — Measuring Implications in Technical Chart Patterns (pp. 469–472)

Answers the inevitable "how far do you think it will go?" — honestly. "You don't know. Nobody knows."

- **Reactions** can be estimated "fairly closely seven or eight times out of ten" from the basic trendline, the parallel
  projection, or the support level. **Moves in the direction of the trend cannot.** A bullish move "may, and often does,
  overrun the upper trendline by running up as far again"; a bearish move may drop "apparently without limit."
- The reason is structural: the overrun **is itself an unreasonable move** — public participation, "the surge of
  uncontrolled speculation (and very often, it is the final surge of that particular trend)." On the downside it is
  panic, "and being completely beyond reason, it follows no rule and knows no predetermined limits."
- This is why the book prefers **nearby progressive stops for taking profits** over limit orders at trendlines or
  resistance levels — and why protective stops exist at all.

**The measurements that do work** (collected)

| Pattern | Minimum expected move |
|---|---|
| Symmetrical Triangle | At least the height of the triangle measured along its **first reaction**; trend logic suggests as much as the move preceding it |
| Right-Angle Triangle | The long side (the first reaction) as a rough minimum |
| Rectangle | Its height, projected from the breakout |
| Head-and-Shoulders | Head-top to neckline, projected from the neckline break — "most emphatically … only a minimum" |
| Flag / Pennant | **"The Flag flies at half mast"** — the pre-flag move duplicated. "Carries with it a most explicit measuring rule, and one which works out with amazing accuracy" |
| Runaway / Continuation Gap | Marks approximately the halfway point of the move |

- Caution on flags: the move is "so spectacularly profitable when it works out" that you will be tempted to buy every
  consolidation after a sharp rise. Wait for the clear example — "a nearly vertical, almost unbelievable rise, followed
  by several days of congestion with practically no volume." **If the congestion continues or sags for more than about
  3 weeks, sell; it is probably not the real thing.**
- Some H&S patterns implying only three or four points "have marked the start of a decline eventually running to
  hundreds of points."
- "In general, however, the best measuring devices are your trendlines, Support-and-Resistance Levels, and the
  all-important signals of increased volume."
- Bassetti is sharper still: "I have always been extremely chary of measuring moves… Always better to observe closely
  what the situation is when the price arrives at the measured point. **Decisions should always be made in the here and
  now, not in the I measured it then.**"

## Chapter 33 — Tactical Review of Chart Action (pp. 473–500)

The book's operations manual: a pattern-by-pattern statement of what to do, in each case split by whether you already
hold a position or not. Opens with the Dow Theory implication.

**Dow Theory implications.** The long record shows substantial theoretical profits on major signals; therefore "the
probabilities favor buying or selling a **majority** of stocks in line with the Major Trend of the Averages… The line of
'most probable gain' is the line of the Major Trend." Restated plainly: *we do not try to sell stocks at the top in a
bull market; we do not try to pick up bargains at the bottom in a bear market; we do not deliberately buck a trend that
history shows is likely to continue.*

- This edition softens the absolutism: counter-trend trades *are* allowed where an individual chart justifies them —
  but "cautiously and with a full realization that the majority of stocks are moving in a contrary manner," and best
  understood as **partial hedges**. Example: in a mature bull market, hold three-quarters of capital long and short a
  limited number of individually weak stocks; if the bull continues, the shorts close for losses that are "the
  reasonable cost of 'insurance'"; if the trend reverses, they cushion the longs until the reversal signal.
  (Bassetti develops this into **"natural hedging"**, insisting profits should be sought on *both* sides.)
- The **Evaluative Index** (Ch 38) lets you say "the market appears to be about 60% Bullish" instead of simply bullish or
  bearish, so you can "roll with the punch" rather than taking an all-out position. (Bassetti: **"Rhythmic Trading."**)
- **Summary rule**: "do not make a majority of your commitments against the Major Trend. During periods of potential
  Reversal, gradually reduce your long holdings, and make short sales to a limited amount in weak stocks; but do not
  attempt to anticipate either a Major Top or Major Bottom in the Averages by making an all-out commitment counter to
  the main trend."

**Pattern-by-pattern tactics**

- **Head-and-Shoulders Top** — (A) If long: on a neckline break closing ≥3% below, place a stop ⅛ below the last close
  next morning, repeating daily until caught. (B) Short sales after the breakout, on a recovery of **40%** of the
  distance from the right-shoulder top to the breakout low, *or* on a rally to a line drawn down across the head and
  right shoulder, *or* on a pullback to the neckline — **whichever comes first.** If the breakout continues for several
  days, compute the 40% on the whole move.
- **Head-and-Shoulders Bottom (Kilroy Bottom)** — mirror image, requiring **increased volume** on the neckline
  penetration; new purchases on a 40% reaction (which must be on *decreasing* volume), or to the head/right-shoulder
  line, or on a throwback to the neckline.
- **Complex/Multiple H&S** — same tactics as the simple form.
- **Rounding Tops and Bottoms** — "difficult to set precise rules." If long into a rounding top, sell at market as soon
  as the picture looks definite. A short sale of a rounding top "could be very profitable" but demands certainty: well
  formed, following a long rise, weeks in the making, protected by a stop above the top of the curve (no basing points
  are available). For bottoms: buy not on the first spasm of activity out of the saucer but **on the reaction following
  it, "which would almost surely come."**
- **Symmetrical Triangles** — (A) If committed: you are "locked" in and cannot safely sell, since the odds favour a
  breakout in your direction. On a favourable breakout, close for profit per the standard rules and **mark it as a rebuy
  on the next reaction**. On an adverse breakout close, use tight ⅛-point progressive stops. (B) If not committed:
  **stay away until a clear, definite breakout close**, then buy the next reaction (if up and the major trend is up) or
  short the next rally. **Avoid breakouts that come far out toward the apex; the most reliable occur about two-thirds
  along.**
- **Right-Angle Triangles** — same rules; early breakouts more dependable; volume confirmation matters on ascending
  upside breaks and is not strictly required on descending downside breaks. Because they carry a directional
  implication, entries *within* the pattern are possible — but since the flat side represents supply/demand of unknown
  magnitude, "it might be better policy to note such formations in the making, and wait until the decisive breakout."
- **Broadening Tops** — you shouldn't be long one; the early reversals and trendline rules would have removed you.
  After completion they "offer excellent opportunities for a short sale": after a downside close below the fourth
  reversal point, sell short on a rally of about **40%** of the distance from the fifth reversal (the extreme top) to the
  breakout low, with the stop above that extreme top.
- **Rectangles** — (A) If committed: there are no volume signals or breakouts early on, but once the pattern is
  established (four reversals defining top and bottom) you may trade the extremes. Since continuation is the odds-on
  outcome, you will probably **pass up the fifth reversal** and ride along. (B) If not committed: trade the fifth and
  subsequent reversals; better, **wait for the sixth reversal**, which aligns you with continuation. Short on any
  downside breakout close; buy on an upside breakout close with increased volume — both executed on the corrective move
  after the breakout.
- **Double/Multiple Tops and Bottoms** — (A) If long: on penetration and close below the extreme bottom between the
  tops, sell on ⅛-point progressive stops. (B) If short: on a close above the intervening high, cover on tight stops.
  (C) If uncommitted: treat that penetration as the reversal signal and commit on the following rally/reaction.
- **Right-Angled Broadening Formations** — handle breaks through the horizontal side like multiple tops/bottoms and
  right-angle triangles.
- **Diamond** — if you are sure it is valid, trade it exactly like a symmetrical-triangle breakout: wait for a definite
  break, and hold existing commitments until it comes.
- **Wedges** — no special rules needed within the pattern; the trendline and support/resistance rules remove you once
  convergence is clear, and at worst your stops do. New purchases from a falling-wedge breakout expect **increased
  volume**; short sales from a rising wedge need less pronounced volume. Enter on rallies/reactions after a clear
  breakout close beyond the wedge lines.
- **One-Day Reversals** — not tradable patterns in the book's sense; "mainly useful as a gauge in helping to find the
  precise Top or Bottom of a Minor Move in order to protect profits." The one-day reversal, the exhaustion gap and the
  exceptionally-heavy-volume day are each worth watching; **any two together carry more weight than one; all three carry
  very strong implications.** Trading the one-day moves themselves "would lie almost in the field of gambling."
- **Flags and Pennants** — the decline from a flag often brings the price back to a normal entry (the Red Trendline, the
  Blue Parallel, or a 40–50% correction of the mast). If the mast is the first such move out of a level or moderately
  rising trend and the major trend is bullish, **buy at the first opportunity (the Blue Parallel)** because the reaction
  is sometimes very brief. **Volume must drop off sharply and stay slight**; any increase casts suspicion on the whole
  pattern, so place a tight ⅛-point stop under the close of any day inside the flag that shows notably increased volume —
  you are taken out at once if it fails, and almost certainly left in if the breakout is under way (often with a
  breakaway gap). Horizontal-topped flags are rectangles: commit on the sixth reversal or almost anywhere in the pattern,
  since it cannot last long. Beyond 3 weeks, set stops at the computed distance outside the extremes. And "it is
  particularly hard to give up hoping with this kind of pattern" — hence the time limit.
  If you are long when a flag starts to form, **hold**; and if you were stopped out at 30 after a run from 20 to 32 and
  then see a drastic (unmistakable) drying-up of volume, "you are justified in buying right back in again, even at a
  higher price than you received only a few days before."
- **Gaps** — a high-volume upside breakaway gap out of a pattern or dull area is bullish: hold until exhaustion signs,
  a volume reappearance, another gap or a one-day reversal, then protect with tight progressive stops. Decide whether a
  mid-move gap is continuation (hold for an equal further move, watching closely near the objective) or exhaustion
  (protect at once with a tight progressive stop). Reverse for shorts, remembering downside breakaways needn't carry
  volume. **An adverse breakaway gap out of a pattern closes the commitment immediately at market or on tight stops.**
- **Support and Resistance** — "When you are long a stock, you do not want to see it violate any Minor Bottoms
  previously made. Neither do you want to see it violate any of the preceding Minor Tops which it has surpassed."
  Use **both** as basing points (they usually coincide). Intermediate tops/bottoms set the objectives of intermediate
  moves. Multiple tops are support; multiple bottoms are resistance; an H&S neckline is one or the other; a symmetrical
  triangle's **apex is a strong support/resistance point** that can reassert itself on a later move.
- **Trendlines** — covers the hard case: a stock acting badly that has neither broken a pattern nor violated a minor
  peak, and whose reaction runs on without producing any basing point. Then **use the points of contact with the basic
  trendline as emergency substitutes for minor bottoms**: place the stop at the computed distance below the last point at
  which the stock touched the bottom trendline and moved decisively away from it. On penetration and close below that
  point, sell on tight progressive stops. Also: "the changes of angularity and direction in Intermediate trendlines are
  helpful in showing the gradual turning of a Major Trend."

## Chapter 34 — A Quick Summation of Tactical Methods (pp. 501–504)

The single most quotable page in Part 2: two checklists built on one distinction.

> **"The signal that shows weakness or failure of a move in one trend is not always a signal to make new commitments on
> the opposite side of the market. More often than not, in fact, it is nothing of the kind."**

**Get out of present commitments** on:

- Adverse breakout from a Head-and-Shoulders formation
- Adverse breakout from a Symmetrical Triangle
- Adverse breakout from a Rectangle
- Establishment of a new minor low (or new minor high) in the adverse direction
- Adverse breakout from a Diamond
- Adverse breakout from a Wedge
- A One-Day Reversal, **if** marked by heavy volume or a gap
- Adverse breakout from a Flag or Pennant
- Clear penetration of any resistance or support level in the adverse direction
- An adverse Breakaway Gap
- Appearance of an **Island** after a move in the favourable direction
- Penetration of a basic trendline **in the absence of pattern or other favourable criteria**

> *All breakouts must **close** in the breakout area; a close 3% beyond the support, trend or pattern gives the danger
> signal; all exits are executed with **⅛-point progressive stops**.*

**Make new commitments** on:

- Alignment with the major Dow trend — or, to a limited extent, counter-trend as insurance to reduce overall risk
- Breakout from a Head-and-Shoulders pattern
- Breakout from a Symmetrical Triangle, **provided it is not working into the final third toward the apex**
- Breakout from a Right-Angle Triangle
- Breakout from a Rectangle, or *(possibly)* on points of contact beginning with the **sixth** reversal
- Breakout from a Broadening Top
- Breakout from a Double or Multiple Top/Bottom — i.e. through the valley floor between tops, or the dome between bottoms
- Breakout from a Wedge, or *(possibly)* within the wedge in its last third as it approaches the apex
- Flags and Pennants, after sufficient corrective move — or *(possibly)* within the pattern if volume and all other
  indications strongly confirm it
- Clear penetration of a well-defined support or resistance area
- A Breakaway Gap *(possibly)* — though "it is extremely difficult to catch Breakaway Gaps, and we would not recommend
  this as a general practice"
- After formation of an important, well-defined **Island** following a considerable move
- Contact with, or penetration of, the "favourable" trendline **if both trendlines are moving in the major-trend
  direction** (Blue top trendline in a bull market, Red bottom trendline in a bear market)

> *Breakouts must show a close in the breakout area, conform to volume requirements, and satisfy the 3% rule. The items
> marked "possibly" are the ones requiring exceptional care. **All commitments except those noted are made on the next
> following reaction or rally. All commitments are protected by stops from the moment they are made. Stops are moved as
> conditions justify — always in the favourable direction, never in the adverse.***

The chapter also names the three tactical operations: getting in; getting out of a commitment that worked; and getting
out of one that didn't. The first and third are the hard ones — profit-taking "seldom present[s] very difficult problems,
since the picture has developed normally and in the way you hoped."

## Chapter 35 — Effect of Technical Trading on Market Action (pp. 505–506)

Answers the reflexivity objection: does charting create the patterns? "This does not seem to be true."

- The patterns predate the chartists; today's charts follow the old patterns, and regulation and margin changes altered
  them "only in degree and not in their fundamental nature."
- The market is too big for anyone to control, and "extremely democratic in the sense that it represents the integration
  of the hopes and fears of many kinds of buyers and sellers" — investors, industrialists, employees, long-term holders,
  and short-term traders who trade on fundamentals, tips, hunches, the stars, or inside knowledge.
- **Technicians are a minority.** "The cold attempt to analyze a situation on the basis of the market record alone does
  not appeal to many people. Technical analysis leaves out the warmth and human interest of the boardroom … the
  whispered information on new patents, and the thrilling study of the quarterly earnings reports." (Bassetti: "unless I
  am mistaken the only appearance of irony in Magee's work.")
- Why patterns persist: "the same kinds of events produce the same kinds of emotional responses, and hence, the same
  kinds of market action. These characteristic approaches are extremely durable… it is very difficult to change the
  perceptive habits of a lifetime."
- Bassetti's modern qualification: professionals now actively study what makes systems work and act to frustrate them —
  hunting stops above a congestion zone to force a breakaway *or* to create a bull trap; and the proliferation of
  systems trend-followers in futures has arguably created conditions hostile to them as a group. "The moral of the story
  is that the trader/investor must be ever alert for the false move and the changing rhythm of the markets."
- "No one has been able to quantify chart analysis, nor to disguise his own activities from the x-ray of the charts. Nor
  has anyone changed human nature to eliminate treachery and perfidy and truculent defense of self-interest."
- 9th-ed. footnote: in 2005 Smith Barney fired its entire technical-analysis staff — "In my view an unintended
  validation of the craft. When you have to shoot the messenger it says something about the state of the market."

## Chapter 36 — Automated Trendline: The Moving Average (pp. 507–512)

The honest appraisal. Back in 1941 the authors thought averaging the data would yield "a sort of Automated Trendline"
that interpreted trend changes mechanically. "It seemed almost too good to be true. **As a matter of fact, it was too
good to be true.**"

**The two structural defects**

1. **It cannot escape its past.** "The smoother the curve (longer cycle) one has, the more 'inhibited' it is in
   responding to recent important changes of trend."
2. **"The tail tends to wag the dog."** Large figures from the start of the window — six months or a year ago — may
   unduly affect the present average and "may conceal or mask some important feature by distorting the curve."

**Construction.** Simple, Weighted/Exponential, and Linear. The authors prefer **simple** ("the simple methods work just
as well and sometimes better"); the others are more useful with computers. Most common: **50-day and 200-day**. To
increase sensitivity, shorten to 10 or 20 days — or increase lead time by starting on the third day of a 10-day average,
the 20th day of a 50-day. Running-total shortcut: drop the oldest day, add the newest. A 200-day average can equivalently
be built from one day a week over 30 weeks.

**Crossovers and penetrations.** General rule: treat the price line crossing the average as a signal in the direction of
the crossing.

- *Uptrends* — hold longs while price stays above the average. Price intersecting/penetrating upward = buy. Price going
  above the 200-day then falling sharply toward it **without** penetrating = buy. Price falling below the average while
  the average is still rising = possible buy. Price spiking down too fast and far below a *declining* average = expect a
  short-term rebound toward the line: **a possible whipsaw trap**.
- *Downtrends* — hold shorts while price stays below the average. Price moving above the average while it is still
  falling = sell. Price moving below, rising toward it, failing to penetrate, and breaking down again = sell. Price
  rising too fast above a rising average = expect a short-term reaction; could be a whipsaw. And penetration of the
  average occurring in close conjunction with a trendline penetration is a buy or sell by direction.
- *Sideways movements* — if fluctuations are broad relative to the average's length, price will oscillate back and forth
  across it.
- *Gaps* — moving averages tend to be penetrated in close proximity to a **breakaway gap**, particularly at the start of
  a major phase of an intermediate cycle and where breakaway gaps begin correction phases.

**The area-pattern pitfall.** "Normally, the Moving Average oscillates through the center of these areas producing buy
and sell signals in rapid succession. In Area Patterns, the Moving Average is a headache to the trader because he never
knows which penetration is the one preceding either the renewal of the trend or Confirmation of a Reversal." In
triangles the average runs through the centre — but the technician has a small edge: **the penetration that occurs when
the triangle reaches its apex and prices break out "is likely to be the most important one"** during the sideways phase,
and such penetrations often coincide with a trendline penetration.

- Verdict: "As a price derivative product, the Moving Average can be a trend indicator by the way it fits a trendline.
  But, nevertheless, it should be considered an **adjunctive tool** to everything else you have learned."
- Bassetti's chart note on the 150-day average: trading its crossovers gave "somewhat the same advantage that trading
  the Dow Theory afforded" up to the 1980s — then "the system stopped working. But here in 1929 it would have been a
  life saver."

## Chapter 37 — "The Same Old Patterns" (pp. 513–576)

The book's demonstration chapter and its longest: ~60 pages of annotated charts whose only argument is that the patterns
recur. "There are men who have observed the market long enough and carefully enough to discover that there are not quite
so many unexpected events as the newcomer might be led to believe."

- The thesis in one sentence: "**it is possible to be wrong part of the time, and still to be successful on the
  balance**" — all that is required is enough experience to know what usually happens, how often the unexpected occurs,
  and how to deal with it when it does. "These are the same general problems that would confront the space traveler, the
  chemist, the physician, or almost anyone else in his daily affairs."
- Most of the book's charts are the same ones used in the 1947 first edition — 1928-29, the 1930s, the 1940s — and this
  chapter adds examples from 1947–1966 (5th ed.), through 2000 (8th ed.) and up to 2005–06 (9th ed.). "It would be
  possible to include ten times the number of good examples."
- Representative pairings include two 1956 charts that are near mirror images — Granite City Steel ascending in
  support/resistance steps and Masonite descending in exactly the same way, with rallies stopping at previous bottoms at
  44, 41, 38 and 36.
- Caution restated in context: a trendline break at the end of such a decline, "utterly lacking" volume, means no more
  than a secondary recovery.
- **The 9th edition's two most substantive additions** are at the end of the chapter:
  - **Fig. 275 — the Basing Point procedure on weekly bars**, applied to the NASDAQ 100: substitute "three-bars-away"
    for "three-days-away" and the method gets long in 1991, holds roughly ten years, reverses in ~2001, reverses again
    at the 2002 bottom, and is long into 2006. Offered as an **objective replacement or augmentation of Dow Theory for
    the very long-term investor.**
  - **Fig. 276 — the full procedure including new-high stop advances**, which in the Apple case stopped the trader out
    *sooner* than wave-low basing points. The trade-off is explicit: "A mid-trend blowoff might cause a premature exit
    … unless the position were constructed with scale-in scale-out units as recommended in Chapter 42." The same chart
    shows "the result of not honoring stops, as Apple falls off the table in the Reagan Crash of 1987."

## Chapter 38 — Balanced and Diversified (pp. 577–584)

Attacks the demand for a single answer to "What do you think of the market?" The averages are **abstractions**, "not
railroads, manufacturing companies, airlines." It is true that when the averages advance a majority of stocks advance;
it is **not** reversible — and the reversal leads to the fallacy that a stock which has *declined* in a bull market is
therefore attractive because "it must catch up with the others."

**The statistical demolition.** Of nearly 700 listed stocks studied around 1929:

| Group | Count |
|---|---|
| Made bull-market highs **before** 1929 | 262 |
| Topped in 1929 but **before August** | 181 |
| Went into new bull-market high ground **after** 1929 and before mid-1932 | 44 |
| Made their highs in **Aug/Sep/Oct 1929** and crashed in Oct–Nov | **184** |

> **"In other words, only 27% of the stocks acted the way everybody knows all stocks acted."**

(Chrysler peaked in October 1928 and had fallen from 140 to 60 before the 1929 panic. Bassetti adds that the same held
in 1999–2000, and that in the post-March-2000 decline the Dow held up far better than the NASDAQ and S&P, which fell
~50%.)

**The Magee Evaluative Index (MEI)** — the solution, and one of the book's genuinely original tools.

- **Construction**: keep daily charts on (say) 100 stocks. At the end of each week mark each chart **+** or **−**
  according to your judgment of whether it is in a bullish or bearish *major* trend. Total them. If 75 are plus, "the
  market looks 75% Bullish to you." Rising percentage = strengthening; falling = weakening.
- It is explicitly **not** a precise tool: "it gives no positive signals; and, in the final analysis, it is a reflection
  of the judgment and opinion of the person who is maintaining it." There is no point at which it says "sell everything."
  "The Index will float and adjust itself continually to the shifting conditions."
- **Use 1** — scale total commitments to the reading: a market where 53% of stocks are bullish is not as strong as one
  where 80% are, so larger long commitments (greater total risk) are justified in the latter. This lets the investor
  "roll with the punch" and be "almost automatically … withdrawn from a deteriorating market before things became too
  dangerous… without the need for torturing decisions as to whether to sell now or wait a while."
- **Use 2 (the extension)** — proportion capital between long and short. With the Index near 50%, select several
  stronger-than-average stocks to buy and several weaker-than-average to short, balancing total risk. A sweeping advance
  produces losses on the shorts offset by larger gains on the longs — "the price you have paid to be in a protected
  position." A sudden collapse works the other way. "It is also quite possible, in a more normal market, that both the
  long positions and the short positions will show gains."
- What this amounts to: **"a systematic and continuous arbitrage or hedge"** — as the Index rises, reduce shorts and
  increase longs; as it falls, the reverse. (Bassetti names this the **Natural Hedge**, implemented by **Rhythmic
  Trading**: long the DIA in a bull market, short the weak Dow components — or better, short stocks positively correlated
  to the weak components, since even weak Dow members are cushioned by passive indexers.)
- "This method is essentially conservative. Those who have always feared the short sale as a purely speculative gamble
  might well reexamine short selling from the standpoint of using the short sale as a regular part of their investment
  program as counterbalance to the long holdings." Its primary aim is **protection of capital**; by its nature it
  eliminates plunging for spectacular profits — and "it makes it possible to eliminate a large part of the anxiety and
  uncertainty that so many traders and investors carry every day and often late into the night."
- **As an overbought/oversold indicator** — Bassetti appends Magee's market letter of 28 September 1985: the MEI had
  fallen to **9% Strong**. "For over 20 years, all major stock market Bottoms have corresponded with extremely low MEI
  readings" — ≤5% Strong through the turbulent 1965–82 period, then 9% at the June 1982 low that preceded the explosion
  from 770 to ~1300, and 8% at the February and June 1984 double bottom that preceded the run from 1079 to 1372. The
  letter correctly read the slightly-higher bottoms as evidence of a "renewed upswing" secular regime, in which "stock
  market Bottoms will tend to be less severe and Tops more extremely overbought."

## Chapter 39 — Trial and Error (pp. 585–586)

Short and about method, not markets. "You will not expect to turn in a perfect record from the start. You may indeed do
poorly."

- **Back-test your refinements against your own chart records** before risking capital on them. The cited case: a trader
  with a poor record through a fast bear phase rechecked 30 actual trades against methods he developed afterward; where
  the original showed a loss at ~40% a year on the capital employed, the revised method would have produced a profit at
  an annual rate of **156%**. "Such a result, while not conclusive, would strongly suggest trying out the new method in
  all similar situations in the future."
- "It is only by continual checking and testing that you can learn to pick up more of the profitable opportunities and
  protect yourself better against the unexpected Reversals."
- "You are not gambling blindly in this work; you are intelligently using past experience as a guide — and it is a
  dependable guide."
- The defensive value: technical knowledge "will save you from 'buying at the top' in the final Climactic Blow-off, and
  it will save you from selling everything in a fit of depression and disgust when the Bottom is being established."
- Bassetti's version: "if their knowledge of this material does nothing more than keep them from making **stupid
  mistakes** like buying a top or buying a downtrend or buying before a bottom has completed forming, then their time
  will have been well spent."

## Chapter 40 — How Much Capital to Use in Trading (pp. 587–590)

The turn from points and percentages to dollars. "Just as an understanding of the technical signals and patterns alone
will not guarantee your profits without a tactical method of applications, so your tactics alone will not insure you
profits until you have tailored your method to fit your pocketbook."

- **Timeline**: no capital at the start. Any commitment in the first 4–5 weeks of a new chart "would be no more than
  gambling on a hunch." Expect about **two months of thankless charting** before any clear technical picture; first
  trades should be theoretical.
- **The apprenticeship**: "it usually takes about 2 years to gain enough practical experience to operate safely," during
  which many traders gradually lose their capital and retire permanently, "leaving their money behind them." Therefore
  experiment mostly on paper and commit only a small part of actual capital, so that after two years you either have
  consistent (even small) profits and can scale up, or you have identified your mistakes without losing your reserve.
- **Frame it as tuition**: money you could afford to spend for experience — comparable to a course of instruction,
  improving a property, or the salary forgone in taking a lower-paid job in a new field.
- **Budget outside the trading capital**, "even if that calls for trimming your budget to make that possible." The
  purpose is freedom: "free from any pressure to take unnecessary risks, free from the need to sell stock prematurely to
  meet obligations, and free from heckling fears and worries."
- "In no case do you want to risk everything you can scrape together on the theory that here is the quick way to make
  easy money. That simply is not true, and the chances are overwhelmingly against you."
- "The important thing at the start is not how many dollars you can make, but **what percentage of increase per year**
  you can average with the capital you are using."
- Bassetti's blunt addition: "this question begs the question. It presupposes that the reader *has* capital. If the
  reader does not have capital, but is gambling with the milk money or the mortgage payment, his failure is virtually
  assured. **Do not speculate with money whose loss will occasion you more than passing discomfort.**"

## Chapter 41 — Application of Capital in Practice (pp. 591–596)

Asset allocation, derived from six agreed propositions:

1. Major trends run long and cover many points.
2. Almost unbelievable profits could be made by buying the exact bottom and selling the exact top.
3. **It is not possible to do either.**
4. It *is* possible to avoid being trapped in purchases near a bull top (or short sales near a bear bottom) so that
   losses become ruinous.
5. It *is* possible to profit by trading with the major trend, on intermediate corrections, and occasionally on a stock
   moving contrary to the major trend.
6. The greatest and most dependable profits come in **the principal period of advance (or decline) — not in the earliest
   phases, nor in the rounding-off near the end.**

> **Therefore: a relatively small equity at the very start of a major move, very little at or near its end, and a very
> substantial interest during the mid-portion when the move is making the greatest headway.**

- The authors wanted to express this as an equation and conclude they cannot: "the idea of a Major Trend is, itself, a
  matter of definition," and the trend is an abstraction from many individual stocks. The **Evaluative Index** gives an
  approximation good enough for practical purposes. (Bassetti makes the allocation explicit: if 30% of stocks are
  bullish, 30% bearish and 40% neutral, commit 30% long, 30% short, 40% cash — assuming the long and short risks are
  balanced.)
- **The proportion of capital used is not the whole measure of participation.** Price level and margin change the risk.
  $8,000 of a conservative preferred bought outright might gain or lose 4–5% in a great market move; $8,000 of options
  on a low-priced speculative stock might produce several hundred percent or a total loss. Status can be varied by
  changing the total commitment, switching between more and less speculative or higher- and lower-priced stocks, or
  varying margin. These three factors — **sensitivity, price, and margin** — combine into the **Composite Leverage
  Index** (Appendix A).
- Applying a specific leverage at a specific time, however, "must be a matter of experience, or intuition based on
  experience." Keep the shape of a major swing in mind: bull markets start moderately and accelerate toward the top;
  bear markets move fastest at the start and taper; bear markets are steeper.
- **A rotation rule**: high-grade issues and market leaders start early in a bull market and advance steadily, topping
  with a reversal pattern, then declining more steeply than they rose. Low-priced, low-grade issues stay dormant early,
  then skyrocket to a top that is often *months later* than the conservative group's, and then drop very fast.
  **Therefore concentrate bull-market trading in high-grade stocks early and low-grade stocks late**; in a bear market,
  short high-grade stocks even while the "cats and dogs" are still running up, then switch the shorts to the low-grade
  issues when their reversal is signalled.
- The chapter closes with a short history of options (Mediterranean "to arrive" agreements ~2,000 years ago; the CBOE's
  1973 standardisation) and Bassetti's tally of derivatives disasters as a warning: Bank Negara −$5bn (1992–93), Showa
  Shell Seikiyu −$1.58bn, Metallgesellschaft −$1.34bn, Barings −$1.33bn on stock index futures; known losses 1987–95
  totalling $16.7bn against an estimated $25tn market. "Perhaps this is sufficient to warn the general investor that the
  field is strewn with financial mines for even the sophisticated."

## Chapter 42 — Portfolio Risk Management (pp. 597–608)

The most heavily revised chapter in the 9th edition: Magee's argument plus Bassetti's modern risk framework.

**Magee's core: the overtrading paradox.** "A series of identical percentage gains and losses on your capital does not
give you a series of equal gains and losses in dollars and cents."

| Risk per trade | $1,000 after 10 losses and 10 gains (any order) |
|---|---|
| 100% | wiped out on the first loss, no matter how many successes precede it |
| **40%** | **under $100** — more than 90% of capital gone |
| **8%** | **$937** — ~94% intact; a handicap of only ~⅓ of 1% per trade |

- 8% "is a fair average figure for short-term transactions resulting in a loss, in fact, a rather liberal figure
  according to extensive tabulations of actual transactions."
- The real-world stress test: one historical period ran **10 months** between the actual market turn and the Dow
  reversal signal. At 30 days per trade, ten successive wrong-way trades at 8% reduce $1,000 to $434 — and the next ten
  winners bring it back to $937. "You could have righted the boat and sailed right on if you were working on the 8%
  basis, whereas if you had been following the 40% basis … you would have been sunk without a trace, a victim of
  overtrading."
- The symmetrical warning against over-caution: you could refine your conservatism until you were right 95% of the time,
  but you would trade so rarely that the profit wouldn't justify the work — "you can obtain nominally respectable returns
  on your capital without very much study and without much risk." Maintaining a chosen Composite Leverage protects
  against *both* over-caution and (more commonly) unconscious overtrading.
- Both pyramiding plans and **averaging-down / scale-up plans** are mentioned — Bassetti's verdict on the latter:
  "Invitations to disaster, both techniques. Avoid such methods."
- Bassetti's historical claim: Magee "could easily be called the father of modern investment theory" — the **Sensitivity
  Index anticipates beta** (systematic risk) and **Composite Leverage precedes modern portfolio risk analysis**, arguably
  more practically.
- **Risk and trend**: risk is not a fixed property. "Risk is diminished to a portfolio and a stock when it is with the
  trend" — demonstrated in Bassetti & Brooker's MTA paper *Dissecting Dow Theory*, which argues risk measurably
  diminishes in the Industrials when the portfolio is aligned with the Dow Theory trend. The Enron case is the converse.

**The statistics to review periodically** (Bassetti's list)

- Original risk per trade · actual realised loss
- Average loss and profit per trade, and their ratio
- Number of winning and losing trades, and their ratio
- **Time** in winning vs losing trades — "long-time trades combined with oversize losses is an ominous sign"
- Equity swings: average drawdown, **maximum drawdown**
- Costs and expenses, total and per trade
- Daily, yearly and catastrophic risk

**VAR, and its limits.** The orthodox procedure — compute returns, build a frequency distribution, derive portfolio
volatility (σ) from correlations, variances and covariances — is sketched and then deflated: "at the end of all this, we
would still be wondering what to do in the Perfect Storm." Reference: Philippe Jorion, *Value At Risk*. Note also that
the **Sharpe Ratio** (Appendix D) "has severe deficiencies in analyzing portfolio risk."

**Pragmatic Portfolio Theory** — Bassetti's simplified alternative, built on the observation that the technician's risk
is *not* the stock's volatility, because he uses a stop. "So our method of risk control is basically more dynamic than
the theory."

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| **Theoretical risk** of one stock | `T$Risk = Volatility × Position × Price` |
| **Operational risk** of one stock | `O$Risk = (Market Price − Stop Price) × Position` |
| **Theoretical portfolio risk** | `TP$Risk = Market Value × Volatility` |
| **Portfolio operational risk** | `PO$Risk = Σ O$Risk (longs) − Σ O$Risk (shorts)` *(summed instead of subtracted under perfect negative correlation)* |
| **Portfolio Risk Factor** | `PRF = PO$Risk ÷ Total Capital` — the risk accepted for one day |
| **Risk over time** | annualise (`Daily Risk × 365`), or collect it from actual daily operations |
| **Catastrophic risk** | the effect on your positions of a 2-, 4-, then 6-standard-deviation move, "when stops will not be honored by specialists and market makers and the market will be stampeding like spooked cattle for the exits" |

- Worked illustration: Microsoft at 120 with annualised volatility 0.44 → theoretical annual risk ≈ $52/share. Those who
  bought at 120 "and who did not have the technician's ability to set a stop and discipline to stick to it saw a risk of
  50%" as it halved to 60 by June 2000.
- **Customising volatility** — compute a stock's volatility *as experienced by your own system*, and banish misbehaving
  stocks "to the portfolios of mutual fund managers." He notes the literature doesn't mention this.
- **Maximum drawdown** via a **wave chart**: measure top of wave to bottom. It tells you how much capital you need and
  how much reserve to keep, and "gives us a vivid depiction of our results." Rule of thumb: when building a system
  without live experience, **multiply maximum drawdown by 3 or 4** to size the backing capital.
- **Controlling the risk**: the greatest danger lies in the greatest leverage — "so the greatest risk is in short options
  — and usually **short puts**" (the $57MM client again). "The least risk lies in being hedged." And consistently using
  the MEI to balance the portfolio reduces both ordinary and extraordinary risk: "In fact, we may profit from an event
  which disemploys many professional money managers."

**The summary money-management procedure**

> `Entry Price − Stop Price = $R1` (Dollar Risk 1)
> `Risk Control Factor × Total Capital = RpT` (Risk per Trade) — e.g. 3% × $100,000 = $3,000
> `$R1 ÷ RpT = TS` (Trade Size)

- **Trade size is the basic unit of risk control**: "Regardless of volatility, 500 shares of anything is riskier than
  100 shares."
- Measure operational risk **daily**; divide by total capital; if the factor is too high, look for hedges or positions to
  eliminate, "starting, of course, with those which are underwater."
- **Recompute stops frequently** (daily for a trader) by Basing Points, support/resistance or trendlines. "Money
  management stops, it should be noted, are inherently **less dynamic** than technically placed stops."
- **Panics are not bolts from the blue**: "There is almost always a prepanic phase which the truly alert trader can
  identify … marked by insider and professional selling which creates increasing volume with Reversal Days occurring in
  many stocks and Gaps and Runaway down days. Almost always these conditions will be preceded by many top formations
  among key stocks — double and triple tops and heads and shoulders and v-tops."
- "**Eternal vigilance is the cost of freedom. It is also the cost of investing success.**"

## Chapter 43 — Stick to Your Guns (pp. 609–610)

The closing argument, and it is about character rather than charts. "Any of several different plans of operation, if
followed consistently over a period of years, would have produced consistently a net gain."

- The portrait of the trader without a method: "at the mercy of every Panic, boom, rumor, tip, in fact, of every wind
  that blows… they often drop their good holdings for a loss on a sudden dip or shakeout; they can be scared out of their
  short commitments by a wave of optimistic news; they spend their days picking up gossip … and they spend their nights
  weighing and balancing, checking and questioning, in a welter of bright hopes and dark fears."
- With no danger points he lets stocks "run entirely past the red light" waiting for "a chance to get out even, a chance
  that often never comes." And a *profit* leaves him no happier, since he has no rule for taking it — so he gets out too
  soon or overstays.
- **What the chart can and cannot do**: "The chart action of a stock discounts and records all presently known
  information about that stock… The chart does not and cannot forecast unforeseeable events, matters that are completely
  unknown to anybody." If you are not satisfied the charts are dependable in a majority of cases, "you should study
  further, or else plan not to use charts at all."
- **"But while you are following any set of rules and policies, follow them to the letter. It is the only way they can
  help you."**
- The three concrete advantages: (1) no single commitment can wipe you out; (2) you will never be frozen in a market
  that turned against you with your capital tied up and unusable in the reversed trend; (3) "you can make your decisions
  calmly, knowing exactly what you will be looking for as a signal to take profits, and knowing also that your losses,
  at the very worst, will be limited to a certain definite amount."
- "All of this means that you will have **peace of mind**… In neither case will you have to take your notebooks home and
  lie awake worrying."
- The speculator's social function, as Magee saw it: "to provide liquidity in the market, and to counteract the
  irrational excesses of market-in-motion. Part of that job is to keep yourself free to become liquid whenever it is
  necessary … And to keep yourself free from irrational and excessive emotional actions."
- Bassetti's sign-off names the 9th edition's new weapons: the Turtle trading manual, the new Basing Points lessons, and
  the refreshed Dow Theory analyses. "Good trading and stick to all your guns!"

# Appendices

## Appendix A, Chapter A — The Probable Moves of Your Stocks (7th ed. Ch 24) (pp. 611–612)

Magee's original full method for computing the **Sensitivity Index** by hand, preserved for the manual chartist (and
declared unnecessary reading for anyone with a computer).

- Mark each chart sheet's high-close and low-close in the margin; measure each stock's range **in eighths of an inch** on
  ratio paper; average across all charted stocks to get the period's average range.
- Fix a permanent arbitrary "normal" average range from several years of data. Divide it by the new period's average
  range → the **Market Reciprocal** for that period. (A wide, active market gives a small decimal like 0.65; a dull
  market a larger one like 1.49.)
- For each stock: `(its range ÷ the Normal Range-for-Price at that price) × Market Reciprocal` = its **fractional
  Sensitivity Index** for that period. Average the fractional indexes across periods for the long-term index; a single
  period is "unreliable and rather meaningless," but the figure settles down and is then "very reliable in forecasting
  the probable future sensitivity of each stock."
- **The interpretation scale**: ≤0.50 extremely conservative · 0.50–1.00 moderately conservative · 1.00–1.50 speculative
  · >1.50 highly speculative. Observed range: over 2.00 down to 0.20 or less.
- Works on any exchange; average your whole portfolio together to get your own Market Reciprocals.

## Appendix A, Chapter B — A Discussion of Composite Leverage (7th ed. Ch 42) (pp. 613–618)

Magee's single-figure measure of total risk exposure — kept "as is" because "in concept, it certainly has more than
historical value."

> **Single stock:** `Composite Leverage = (S × N × T) ÷ (15.5 × C)`
> **Whole portfolio:** `Σ(S × N × T) ÷ (15.5 × Total Capital or Net Worth)`
>
> where **S** = Sensitivity Index · **N** = Normal Range-for-Price at the entry price · **T** = total net paid/received
> on the original transaction · **C** = capital reserved to finance the commitment (cancels against T for outright cash
> purchases or 100%-margin shorts) · **15.5** = the Normal Range for a stock at 25, an arbitrary fixed reference.

- The dramatic illustration: with 30% margin, $1,000 would buy $3,300 of Standard Gas & Electric $4 Preferred at 25 —
  **Composite Leverage 693%**. An outright $1,000 purchase of Celanese 4¾% 1st Preferred at 106 — **11.8%**. A ratio of
  ~**60 to 1**: "your chances of capital gain or loss were about 60 times as great."
- Purpose: "a means of knowing at least approximately how much risk you are assuming," protecting against both
  inordinate exposure and unduly restricted opportunity. Also useful for characterising an investment account as a whole
  and deciding whether it should be made more conservative or more speculative.
- **Negative Composite Leverage** — counter-trend "insurance" positions (shorts in a bull market, purchases in a bear)
  reduce total Composite Leverage, "which is exactly why they would be used." They cannot cancel it to zero, since these
  are rough approximations of semi-independent variables that make erratic moves — but they do genuinely *subtract*
  risk. (Commodity brokers recognise this by reducing margin on offsetting positions.) Bassetti notes the logical
  endpoint: the **Delta Neutral** options arbitrage made possible by Black–Scholes, on which market makers such as Blair
  Hull built large fortunes.

## Appendix A, Chapters C & D — Normal Range-for-Price Indexes · Sensitivity Indexes of Stocks (pp. 619–628)

Reference tables, reprinted from the 5th edition's appendices.

- **Chapter C** — the **Normal Range-for-Price** table: the average percentage range a typical stock exhibits at each
  price level (the "N" in the Composite Leverage formula; 15.5 at a price of 25, 24 at a price of 5). This is Magee's
  proto-volatility measure.
- **Chapter D** — the **Sensitivity Index** table for several hundred NYSE stocks, computed from each stock's annual
  percentage range and median price over **1964–1973**, adjusted for splits, price level and the overall market range of
  each year via the Market Reciprocal (also tabulated so readers can roll their own). The premise: "Barring major changes
  in corporate structure or in the nature of the company's product, we can assume that the relatively conservative stock
  will usually continue to move conservatively, and that the relatively speculative stock will continue to be subject to
  wide swings."

## Appendix B — The Mechanics of Building a Chart · TEKNIPLAT Chart Paper (pp. 629–638)

Magee's original Chapter 23, a step-by-step manual for hand-charting.

- **Staggered dating**: split your charts into four alphabetical groups with sheet-start dates three months apart (A–C
  January, D–I April, J–R July, S–Z October) so that only a quarter of your sheets run out at a time and "at no time
  will all your charts come to the ends of their sheets simultaneously."
- Setup: company name centred, dates left, ticker right; price scale on the left margin positioned so the **centre of
  the sheet is near the current market price**; volume scale near the bottom; a date scale marking each Saturday along
  the very bottom.
- Pre-printed stock-charting paper makes **every sixth vertical line heavier** to mark the (non-trading) Saturday,
  separating the weeks visually. "It is important to make your first entry on the correct vertical line for the day of
  the week."
- The skill worth cultivating: grabbing **high, low, close and volume in a single glance** rather than scanning back and
  forth — "this knack, when you have mastered it, can cut down the time needed for charting 50% or more."
- Covers holidays and ex-dividend days, what to do **when the chart goes off the paper**, recording your own
  transactions on the chart, and supplementary memoranda.
- **Section 2 — TEKNIPLAT chart paper**: the semi-logarithmic sheet used for most illustrations in the book, with its
  price scales (type RU), centre-line placement procedure, time scale, and ex-dividend/split-up adjustments. This is the
  paper whose geometry underlies the Ch 14 observation that intermediate daily uptrends in 10–50 stocks run at about 30°,
  and the Ch 27 trick of counting small vertical spaces to step off a 5%, 8%, 12% or 15% stop interval "without a
  moment's hesitation."

## Appendix C — Technical Analysis of Futures Charts, Using Number-Driven Methods (pp. 639–668) · *Richard McDermott (7th ed. Ch 16), edited*

The book's one systematic treatment of indicator-based technical analysis, with a standing caveat from Bassetti: "This
Appendix is not intended to be a comprehensive treatment… It is presented to give the reader a brief survey of some of
the methods used by this breed of analysts. The original authors of this work (and the present editor) were and are
chart analysts and practitioners of natural systems. AND I believe that the methods presented here can be better used
when practiced by a competent chart analyst."

**The four stock/futures differences** (restating Ch 16): limited life (so long-term support/resistance has less value);
hedging (so near-term levels mean less, and seasonality matters); volume (no fixed supply; open interest can exceed
deliverable supply); weather.

**Stop-loss orders.** "Any professional commodities trader will acknowledge their importance… The essential stop-loss
order is an insurance policy which should be considered part of the cost of doing business." Of the two imperfect
choices — too close or too far away — too far is usually preferable (too close "leads to frustration and increased
transaction costs"), refined by standard deviation or by support/resistance observation. **"The primary caution is to
avoid switching back and forth between too close and too far away"** — flip-flopping invites whipsawing that cuts
profits both ways. Of the 1990s derivatives disasters: "Where were their stop-loss orders?"

**The indicators**

| Indicator | Essentials |
|---|---|
| **Moving Averages** | Common spans 10/30/50/100/200 days; "there isn't just one 'right' time frame." Price input usually the close, but also (H+L)/2, (C+H+L)/3, (O+C+H+L)/4, weighted (H+L+C+C)/4. **Dual MA crossover** (e.g. **9-day / 18-day**): buy when the fast crosses above the slow *and both are rising*; sell when it crosses below *and both are declining*. "Research shows using two averages tends to be the most effective." The average itself acts as support/resistance; the more times it is touched, the more significant a violation; the longer the span, the more significant a crossover. **Reversals in the direction of a moving average are usually more reliable than a crossover.** Caveat: reliance on trending markets — flat or consolidating markets create false signals, and quick-turning markets leave the average behind. |
| **Weighted / Exponential MA** | Weighted: multiply the n-th observation by n and divide by the sum of the weights (1+2+3+4+5=15 for a 5-day); a signal is given when the weighted average changes direction. Exponential: start from a simple MA and add `exponent × (new − previous)`, exponent = 2 ÷ periods (0.1 for 20 days). Both reverse faster than a simple MA, but "they tend to whipsaw with the market and the smoothing factor is lost." |
| **MACD** (Gerald Appel, 1979) | Line 1 = 12-period EMA − 26-period EMA; line 2 (signal) ≈ a 9-period MA of line 1 (exponential values 0.15, 0.075, 0.20). Plotted as a line oscillator or histogram against an equilibrium line: above = bullish, below = bearish. Buy when the faster line crosses the slower from below; sell on the reverse. **"Beware of mechanically trading every MACD crossover, as it can lead to whipsaws and substantial drawdowns."** Refinements: separate buy-side values (8-period/0.22 and 17-period/0.11) and sell-side; overbought/oversold thresholds (±2.50 on the S&P, ±1.20 on the NYSE Index); **trendlines drawn on the MACD line** — in the worked chart this cut nine trades to three, all on the right side of the market; **divergence** from price; and multi-timeframe confirmation (monthly confirms weekly confirms daily confirms intraday). "MACD is best used as a long-term analysis tool." |
| **Bollinger Bands** (John Bollinger) | Two standard deviations either side of a **20-day** moving average — standard deviations chosen because squaring makes the system responsive to short-term change. Their purpose is **only** to indicate whether prices are relatively too high or too low; **they generate no buy/sell signals alone.** Customisable (2.5σ with a 50-day MA; 1.5σ with a 10-day), with the bands containing most but not all data. Combined with RSI: price touching the **upper** band with RSI **below 70** → the trend continues; touching the **lower** band with RSI **above 30** → the trend continues; upper band with RSI above 70 (approaching 80) → possible reversal down; lower band with RSI below 30 (approaching 20) → possible reversal up. The design philosophy: "to create a system driven by market variables, rather than trying to impose an external discipline on the market. It asks the market what is happening." |
| **Stochastic Oscillator** (George Lane, mid-1950s) | Premise: in an uptrend prices close near their high, and as the trend matures they close further from it. Raw value `= 100 × (last close − 20-day low) ÷ (20-day high − 20-day low)`; **%K** = a 3-day MA of the raw value; **%D** = a 3-day MA of %K. Raw + %K = "fast stochastics"; %K + %D = "slow stochastics." Scale 0–100; >80 strong (closing near highs), <20 strong (closing near lows); >75 overbought, <25 oversold (some use 80/20). **%D is more important than %K**, and when %D turns *before* %K "a slow and steady Reversal is often indicated." Periodicity for commodities: use **half the observed cycle** (a 18-day cycle → 9-day stochastics). **Divergence** between price and the oscillator "is often demonstrating a change in price before price itself is changing." Lane's later corrections: divergence alone is not the only valid signal; congested **"Garbage Tops"** take several periods to turn down; and when they do, they often bottom quickly in **"Spike Bottoms."** Crucially, **0% and 100% are widely misunderstood** — they denote extreme weakness and extreme strength, not floors and ceilings: "Zero is not necessarily a Bottom; in fact, the opposite could be true," and in a sustained move the oscillator can stay pinned for months. "If an unviolated trend is in force, with no Consolidations or Reversal Patterns forming, the stochastic signal is likely false." |
| **Volume and Open Interest** | In futures, "volume cannot push the market because for every buyer there must be a seller," so volume is useful mainly as (delayed) confirmation. Open interest measures money flowing in and out: both sides new → OI rises; one new one old → unchanged; both closing → declines. **The four cases**: (1) prices rise, OI rises = new money, **bullish**; (2) prices rise, OI falls = few new buyers, rallies from short covering, bullish short-term but "it will turn Bearish"; (3) prices fall, OI rises = new money with selling pressure, **bearish**; (4) prices fall, OI falls = shorts holding, longs closing, initially bearish "but can turn Bullish" as momentum slows and shorts cover. "Futures are dependent on new money for price stability and growth." |
| **Wilder RSI** (J. Welles Wilder) | Based on the difference between the average of up closes and the average of down closes (EMAs of gains and losses), scaled 0–100 with keys at **30 and 70**; **14 days** standard. It shows movement not apparent on the bar chart, warns of reversals via **swing failures**, clarifies support/resistance, and gives divergence signals. **Swing failures ("W" formations)**: a spike below 30, a rise, then a *shallower* second spike — a buy is generated when the index rises to the level of the middle of the W; inverted above 70 for sells. Time periods: 6-day for intraday profit-taking after a reading above 75 and a pullback; a very sensitive 3-day for re-entry points as the indicator returns between 30 and 70; a 10-week divergence for financials (good in stock indices, bonds and currencies, less accurate elsewhere). "Relative Strength Indexes have become popular as countertrend oscillators." |
| **Divergence** (generic) | "Probably the most common and significant trend pattern in virtually all technical indicators" — instruments or series that normally move together moving apart, indicating weakness in the current direction, and often signalling a reliable reversal. Applied between the Dow Industrials and Transports, a stock and an index, or price and a 10–14 day RSI. "Traders will often wait for a confirmation of a Divergence before placing their transactions." |

**The closing checklist for commodities/derivatives trading**

- Prices are influenced by external factors — weather, supply, demand.
- Futures trading is mostly hedgers and speculators, on shorter horizons than stocks.
- Futures can have an unlimited number of open contracts, and they expire.
- **Stop-loss orders are essential.**
- **Use more than one indicator** to make a buy or sell decision.
- **Trendlines are the most important patterns** and can be drawn on the *indicators* as well as the prices; divergence
  is among the most reliable signals.
- Chart patterns can be used in combination with other indicators.
- **"Trades are made on prices, not on indicators."**
- Look at the big picture — weekly and monthly trends.
- Time periods can be altered for sensitivity (fewer) or smoothness (more).
- And the key integrative point: "if identical signals do not appear, the validity of those appearing should be
  questioned."

## Appendix E — Original Turtle Trading Systems and Procedures (pp. 681–702) · *Curtis Faith*

The complete Turtle rules, reproduced because they are "the very model of a modern major general's trading notebook."
Bassetti requires all his graduate students to produce a manual like it, and offers this as the model — while warning:
"It would be unspeakably rash of me to recommend the adoption out of the box of this system."

**The six components of a Complete Trading System**

1. **Markets** — what to buy or sell
2. **Position Sizing** — how much
3. **Entries** — when
4. **Stops** — when to get out of a loser
5. **Exits** — when to get out of a winner
6. **Tactics** — how to execute

> "Most 'trading systems' that are sold as complete trading systems do not specifically address the exit of winning
> positions… Any trading system that does not address the exit of winning positions is not a Complete Trading System."

**Markets.** Only the most liquid U.S. futures — CBOT bonds and notes; NY coffee, cocoa, sugar, cotton; CME currencies,
S&P 500, Eurodollar, T-bills; Comex gold, silver, copper; NYMEX crude, heating oil, unleaded gas. No grains (position
limits) and no meats (pit corruption). A Turtle could decline a market, but then had to skip it entirely: "We were not
supposed to trade markets inconsistently."

**Position sizing — the N system.** "Position sizing is one of the most important but least understood components."

- **N** = the 20-day exponential moving average of the **True Range** (now called **ATR**), where
  `True Range = max(H−L, H−PDC, PDC−L)`. It represents the average daily range including opening gaps. (Seed the
  calculation with a 20-day simple average.)
- `Dollar Volatility = N × Dollars per Point`
- **Unit** = `1% of account ÷ (N × Dollars per Point)` — sized so that **1 N of movement equals 1% of account equity**,
  truncated to whole contracts.
- Why: **volatility normalisation** means different trades in different markets have about the same chance of a given
  dollar gain or loss, which is what makes diversification across markets actually work. "Even if the volatility of a
  given market was lower, any significant trend would result in a sizeable win because the Turtles would have held more
  contracts."
- Diversification "is much harder to achieve when using insufficient trading capital" — at $100,000 the example unit
  truncates to a single contract and "the granularity of adjustment is too large."

**Position limits** (the Unit as the measure of risk at four levels)

| Level | Type | Maximum |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Single market | 4 units |
| 2 | Closely correlated markets | 6 units in one direction |
| 3 | Loosely correlated markets | 10 units in one direction |
| 4 | Single direction, long or short | 12 units |

The day after the October 1987 crash the Fed cut rates overnight; the Turtles were loaded long in Eurodollars, T-bills
and bonds and lost **20%–40% of account equity in a single day** — "but these losses would have been correspondingly
higher without the maximum position limits."

**Equity adjustment**: decrease the notional account by **20% for every 10%** drawdown from the year's starting equity,
and trade that reduced size until the starting equity is regained.

**Entries — Donchian channel breakouts.** Enter on the breakout intraday, not on the close or the next open; on an
opening gap through the price, enter on the open.

- **System 1** — a **20-day** breakout. **Skip the signal if the last breakout would have been a winner** (a "losing"
  breakout = price moved **2N against** the position before a profitable 10-day exit); direction of the last breakout is
  irrelevant. If skipped, the **55-day breakout serves as the Failsafe Breakout point** so major moves aren't missed.
- **System 2** — a **55-day** breakout, taken every time regardless of the previous result.
- **Adding units** at **½ N intervals** from the actual fill of the previous order, up to the maximum. "If the market
  moved quickly enough it was possible to add the maximum four Units in a single day."
- **Consistency is the whole game**: "most of the profits in a given year might come from only two or three large
  winning trades… The Turtles with the worst records, and all those who were dropped from the program, failed to
  consistently enter positions when the rules indicated."

**Stops.** "There are old traders; and there are bold traders; but there are no old bold traders."

- Standard: **2 N** below entry for longs, 2 N above for shorts — because 1 N = 1% of equity, so no trade risks more
  than **2%**. As units are added, earlier stops are raised by ½ N so the whole position sits ~2 N from the most recent
  unit.
- **The Whipsaw variant** — stops at **½ N** (½% risk), re-entering a stopped-out unit if the market returns to the
  original entry price. More profitable but harder to execute (many more losses, lower win rate), with the side benefit
  that stops never need moving because total risk never exceeds 2% at four units. "A few Turtles traded this method with
  good success."
- Stop *orders* were generally not placed with brokers (to avoid revealing positions); the price was watched and the exit
  executed on a limit or market order. "These stops were non-negotiable exits… each time, every time, without fail."
- Because stops are N-based they adjust to volatility: more volatile markets get wider stops *and* fewer contracts per
  unit, equalising risk across entries.

**Exits.** "Getting out of winning positions too early, i.e., 'taking a profit' too early, is one of the most common
mistakes when trading trend following systems."

- **System 1 exit**: a **10-day** low (longs) / 10-day high (shorts) exits the entire position.
- **System 2 exit**: a **20-day** low / high.
- The arithmetic of why early exits kill the system: exiting winners at 1 N while losing 2 N "you would need twice as
  many winners to offset the losses."
- "Waiting for a 10 or 20 day new low can often mean watching 20%, 40% even 100% of significant profits evaporate…
  The ability to maintain discipline and stick to the rules during large winning trades is the hallmark of the
  experienced successful trader."

**Tactics.** Prefer **limit orders** placed at the lower end of the random "bounce" over market orders. In **fast
markets** do not panic — wait for the market to trade and stabilise before placing orders, since "market orders placed
into a fast market usually end up getting filled at the highest price of the run-up." On **simultaneous signals** in a
correlated group: **"Buy Strength — Sell Weakness"** — buy the strongest markets, short the weakest, one unit per market
and one contract month only. Strength was judged visually, or by how many N price had advanced since the breakout, or by
`(price − price 3 months ago) ÷ current N` to normalise across markets. Roll expiring contracts a few weeks before
expiry, and only into a contract whose own price action "would have resulted in an existing position."

**Why publishing the rules is safe.** Richard Dennis: *"I always say that you could publish my trading rules in the
newspaper and no one would follow them. The key is consistency and discipline."* Faith's evidence: **many of the Turtles
themselves did not make money** — "This was not because the rules didn't work; it was because they could not and did not
follow the rules." The rules depend on capturing infrequent large trends, so many months (sometimes a year or two) can
pass between winning periods, during which doubt becomes easy. One fired Turtle became convinced there were hidden
secrets "which Rich would not reveal," rather than face that his performance was due to his own doubts. Another failure
mode is well-intentioned tinkering — e.g. adding units more slowly than the rules specify, which *seems* conservative
but may increase the chance a retracement hits the exit stops.

**The remedy**: "it is imperative that you personally conduct research using historical trading data… Get your hands
dirty… It is much easier to weather an 8-month losing period if you know that there have been many periods of equivalent
length in the last 20 years."

**Further study**: trading psychology ("much more about making a sincere and open-minded attempt to understand your own
personal psychology than it is about finding the magic psychology book"); money management; trading research (learn to
program simulations; be skeptical of curve-fitting and over-optimization). Final warning: "The best advice comes from
those who aren't selling it, and who make their money trading." And the closing line: "Those who continue to be
successful will never reach their destination, but will learn to find joy in the journey itself."

## 8. Trendlines and channels

# The ten things the book is actually about

1. **Price contains everything.** The chart is not a prediction device but a record of the only thing that matters — the
   supply–demand balance — and that balance already contains every fundamental fact, plus everything no statistic
   captures. Ask constantly: *what does this action mean in terms of supply and demand?*

2. **Trends persist, and their changes leave shapes.** The entire method rests on one empirical claim and one
   consequence: prices move in trends; trends change only when the supply–demand balance changes; and those changes
   usually leave a recognisable price-and-volume pattern because **distribution and accumulation take time**.

3. **Volume is half the pattern, and it is asymmetric.** "It takes buying to put stocks up, but they can fall of their
   own weight." A low-volume break *down* is trusted; a low-volume break *up* is not. Volume is always *relative* to the
   stock's own recent activity, and a notable increase can mean breakout, climax **or** shakeout — only its relation to
   the price pattern decides which.

4. **Support and resistance reverse roles.** The single most useful non-obvious idea in the book: an old top becomes
   support once surpassed; an old bottom becomes resistance once penetrated. It explains gaps, pullbacks, throwbacks,
   necklines, triangle apexes, and why prices move easily through ranges where nobody has a vested interest.

5. **Everything is a probability, nothing is a certainty.** The book qualifies relentlessly — *usually, ordinarily,
   about two-thirds, nine times out of ten* — and says why: "No two chart patterns are ever precisely alike… It cannot be
   avoided if one wishes to present a true picture of what actually happens." Symmetrical triangles fail often. Double
   tops are rare and late. Major down-trendlines barely work. The method's honesty about its own failure rates is its
   most underrated feature.

6. **The objective is not precision; it is the avoidance of large losses.** "The secret of success lies not in buying at
   the very lowest possible price and selling at the absolute top. It is in the avoidance of large losses." Every
   measuring formula gives a **minimum**; every reversal must "have something to reverse"; and no one buys bottoms.

7. **The stop is the method.** Set it at the moment of commitment, compute its distance from the stock's own price level
   and volatility habit, anchor it to a **basing point** confirmed by the three-days-away rule, raise it as the trend
   advances, and never lower it. Then size the position so that the stop distance equals an acceptable fraction of
   capital. This is where the book quietly becomes a risk-management text.

8. **Overtrading destroys good analysis.** The arithmetic is unforgiving: ten 40% losses and ten 40% gains in any order
   leave less than 10% of capital; at 8% they leave 94%. Correct analysis cannot survive incorrect sizing — "simply by
   unwise overextension of your trading, [you can] prevent even the best technical analysis from producing a net profit."

9. **Act with the trend in degrees, not in binary.** The Magee Evaluative Index is the book's answer to "bull or bear?":
   a market 53% bullish is not a market 80% bullish, and capital should be proportioned accordingly, rolling gradually
   from long to short. Counter-trend positions are legitimate as insurance — never as the majority of commitments.

10. **The binding constraint is the person, not the method.** Magee's closing chapter and Faith's Turtle appendix reach
    the same conclusion from opposite directions. Dennis: *"you could publish my trading rules in the newspaper and no
    one would follow them."* Many Turtles lost money with a profitable system because they could not follow it. Magee:
    *"while you are following any set of rules and policies, follow them to the letter. It is the only way they can help
    you."* Hence the book's real deliverable, repeated in both halves: **peace of mind** — knowing in advance what you
    are looking for, what you will do, and what the worst case costs.

## Appendix — a note on the 9th edition's editorial layer

Bassetti's additions are not cosmetic; three of them change how the book should be read:

- **The averages are now tradable.** Index shares (DIA, SPY, QQQQ), index futures and options on them convert Dow
  Theory from a barometer into an executable strategy — which is why the long/short Dow Theory record ($1.9M vs $39.7K
  buy-and-hold) is presented as a *plan* rather than a curiosity.
- **Basing Points is promoted to a first-class method.** Magee buried the procedure in Chapters 27–28; Bassetti codifies
  it into four rules (Ch 28.1), demonstrates it across a full bull market, and then extends it to weekly bars as an
  objective long-term alternative to Dow Theory (Ch 37, Fig. 275).
- **Chart analysis is positioned as the *supervisor* of mechanical systems, not their competitor.** The repeated
  argument — in Chs 16.1, 17.1, 35, 36 and Appendix C/E — is that number-driven systems are "blind" to the difference
  between a trending and a trading market, and that "the most important lesson the futures trader has to learn from
  Edwards and Magee is the ability to see the character of the market, trading or trending, and then to adjust his
  tactics accordingly."

Three recurring Bassetti renamings worth knowing when reading the 9th edition against earlier ones:

| 9th-edition term | Classical term |

| **Natural Hedge / Rhythmic Trading** | Magee's balanced long/short program driven by the Evaluative Index |
| **Pragmatic Portfolio Theory** | A simplified, stop-based alternative to Composite Leverage and VAR |
