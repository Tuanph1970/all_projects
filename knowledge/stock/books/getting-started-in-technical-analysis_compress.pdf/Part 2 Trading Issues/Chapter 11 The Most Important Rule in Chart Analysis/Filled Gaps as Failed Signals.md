# Filled Gaps as Failed Signals

## Definition

A **gap** normally presages continuation of the trend in the gap's direction. When a gap is **filled** — meaning price retraces back through the empty space — the original gap becomes a **failed signal**, implying a significant move in the opposite direction.

> *"As was detailed in Chapter 5, gaps are normally considered patterns that presage a continuation of the trend in the direction of the gap. When a gap is filled, such a development qualifies the original gap as a failed signal."*
> — Schwager, Ch. 11

---

## Simple Analogy: The Bridge That Collapses

Imagine an army advancing across a river. They build a bridge (the gap) and rush troops across. The gap between the banks is "claimed territory." But then the enemy pushes back and destroys the bridge — the troops are now stranded on the wrong side.

That destroyed bridge is the **filled gap**. The army that crossed (trend followers) is now trapped on the wrong side, and the enemy (the reversal) has momentum plus the psychological advantage of having reclaimed "won" territory.

The wider the river (the wider the gap), the more dramatic it is when the bridge falls.

---

## Two Definitions of "Filled"

Schwager provides two definitions and states his preference:

| Definition | Rule | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Typical** | Intraday price reaches the high (or low for downside gap) of the day before the gap | Earlier signal | More false alarms |
| **Strict (Schwager's preference)** | Close beyond the close of the day before the gap | Fewer false alarms | Slightly later signal |

> *"I prefer a more rigid definition requiring a close below (above, in the case of a downside gap) the close on the day prior to the gap day."*
> — Schwager, Ch. 11

---

## Significance Enhancers

Not all filled gaps carry equal weight. Three factors make a filled gap **more significant**:

### 1. Wide Gap
A particularly wide gap represents stronger conviction behind the original move. When even this level of conviction gets reversed, the failure is more meaningful.

### 2. Breakaway Gap
A breakaway gap occurs at the boundary of a trading range (see [[Ch5 — Chart Patterns]]). This is the most important gap type. When a breakaway gap is filled, it suggests the entire breakout was false — similar in spirit to a [[Bull Traps|bull trap]] or [[Bear Traps|bear trap]].

### 3. Consecutive Gaps Filled
When two or more consecutive gaps in the same direction are both filled, the compounding failure amplifies the signal dramatically. Schwager's cotton example (Figure 11.12) shows two consecutive downside gaps being filled, accurately signaling a major bottom.

## Trading Rules

### Filled Upside Gap → SELL

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Trigger** | Close below the pre-gap close (strict) or intraday touch of pre-gap high (typical) |
| **Direction** | SHORT |
| **Stop / Invalidation** | Close above the gap (or highest gap if consecutive) |
| **Hold** | Until price objective or trend reversal evidence |

### Filled Downside Gap → BUY

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Trigger** | Close above the pre-gap close (strict) or intraday touch of pre-gap low (typical) |
| **Direction** | LONG |
| **Stop / Invalidation** | Close below the gap (or lowest gap if consecutive) |
| **Hold** | Until price objective or trend reversal evidence |

## When This Concept Works Best

- **Breakaway gaps that get filled within days**: Schwager's sugar example (Fig 11.8) — a breakaway upside gap filled in about one week. The faster a "strong" gap is filled, the more bearish the implication
- **Gaps near market extremes**: The October 1993 sugar gap (Fig 11.9) formed one day before the extreme high — filled two days later = very early reversal signal
- **Combined with other failed signals**: In Fig 11.8, the gap was filled on a wide-ranging day — two failed signals coinciding. Confluence amplifies reliability
- **Consecutive gap fills**: Two+ gaps in the same direction both getting filled = compounding failure signal

---

## When to Be Cautious

- **Common gaps**: Gaps within a trading range are less significant when filled — they were never strong continuation signals in the first place. Focus on breakaway and runaway gaps
- **Nearly filled but not quite**: Schwager's Apple example (Fig 11.10) shows a gap nearly filled, then two months of sideways before the complete fill. Near-fills can mislead — wait for the actual fill per your chosen definition
- **Pullback before follow-through**: Schwager's cotton example (Fig 11.12) — the filled gap correctly signaled a bottom, but prices first retreated before advancing. Even valid signals may see a correction first
- **The gap region as a zone, not a line**: Gaps create a zone of "no trading" — price behavior within this zone can be choppy and uncertain

> *"Even valid indications of a failed signal may first witness a price correction before the anticipated price move materializes."*
> — Schwager, Ch. 11

---

## Invalidation Rules

Schwager's invalidation is clean and symmetric:

- **Filled upside gap**: Signal remains in force as long as price does not close **above** the gap (or highest gap if there are multiple)
- **Filled downside gap**: Signal remains in force as long as price does not close **below** the gap (or lowest gap if there are multiple)

## Schwager's Key Examples

### March 1991 Sugar (Figure 11.8)
A breakaway upside gap filled about one week later, on a wide-ranging day. Two failed signals coinciding presaged an extended price slide.

### October 1993 Sugar (Figure 11.9)
An upside gap that formed one day before the extreme high was filled two days later — a very early signal of a major trend reversal.

### Apple Computer (Figure 11.10)
A huge upside gap in August 1997 was nearly filled six days later, then sideways for two months, then completely filled in October 1997. Prices continued to descend.

### August 1992 Crude Oil (Figure 11.11)
A wide downside gap to a new low is filled only two days later — an extremely early signal of a very major trend reversal.

### March 1995 Cotton (Figure 11.12)
Two consecutive downside gaps filled. Correctly signaled a major bottom, but prices first retreated before beginning a sustained advance.

## Gap Types Reference (from Ch 5)

| Gap Type | Description | Significance When Filled |
|---|---|---|
| **Common** | Within a trading range | Low — not a meaningful continuation signal to begin with |
| **Breakaway** | At the edge of a range, starting a new trend | **High** — suggests the entire breakout was false |
| **Runaway** | Mid-trend acceleration | Medium — trend may be losing steam |
| **Exhaustion** | Near the end of a trend | Often expected to be filled — less useful as a failed signal |

## Key Takeaway

Filled gaps are one of the most **time-sensitive** failed signals — they can provide extremely early reversal signals (Schwager's crude oil example: two days after the gap). The concept is straightforward: a gap represents a bold claim by the market ("prices jumped so fast nobody could trade in between"). When that claim is retracted (the gap is filled), the retraction speaks louder than the original claim. Schwager's preference for the strict fill definition (close beyond pre-gap close rather than intraday touch) reflects his general philosophy throughout Ch 11: slightly later confirmation in exchange for fewer false alarms.
