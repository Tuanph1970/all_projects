# Chapter 4 — Trading Ranges and Support and Resistance

> "In a narrow market, when prices are not getting anywhere to speak of but move in a narrow range, there is no sense in trying to anticipate what the next big movement is going to be — up or down."  — Edwin Lefèvre

## Concepts in this chapter

1. [[Trading Range]] — the sideways corridor that markets spend most of their time in
2. [[Trading Range Breakout]] — the moment a range gives way and a new trend may emerge
3. [[Support and Resistance]] — the general principle of price floors and ceilings (and how they swap roles)
4. [[Prior Major Highs and Lows]] — single peaks and troughs that act as future S/R
5. [[Concentrations of Relative Highs and Lows]] — *zones* of S/R formed by clusters of minor extremes
6. [[Price Envelope Bands]] — S/R derived dynamically from a moving average (incl. Bollinger Bands)

## Practical takeaways

- Markets spend **most** of their time in trading ranges, but ranges are the **hardest** environment to trade profitably.
- The single most useful piece of advice: when a range is identified, *consider not trading at all*.
- Confirmation rules (duration, percentage penetration) separate real breakouts from noise.
- Once a breakout sticks, the old boundary **flips role** — the ceiling becomes a floor, the floor becomes a ceiling.
- A single prior peak/low is one data point; a *cluster* of them is a zone, and zones tend to be more reliable than single points.
- Envelope bands work as gauges of overbought/oversold *only in ranging markets*; in a trend, prices can hug one band for weeks.
